# A further assessment of trophic and spatial inter-relations of galaxiids and salmonids in New Zealand

G. J. Glova and P. M. Sagar

## Abstract

The trophic and spatial inter-relations between 2 small native fish (Galaxias vulgaris Stokell and G. paucispondylus Stokell, Galaxiidae) and juveniles of 2 introduced fish (Oncorhynchus tshawytscha Walbaum and Salmo trutta L., Salmonidae) were investigated in a New Zealand stream. Interspecific overlap in timing of feeding among these four fish species was greatest between pre-dusk and pre-dawn (1 730-0530). Aquatic invertebrates were consumed exclusively by all fish species except 0. tshawytscha, which also preyed on Trichoptera imagos, particularly at night. Dietary similarities were least between galaxiids, moderate between galaxiids and salmonids, and greatest between salmonid species. Both galaxiid species were found primarily in riffles, with G. vulgaris occupying slightly deeper (10.3 m) and slower (0.5-0.8 m s-I) waters than did G. paucispondylus (10.2 m deep and 0.7-1 .O m .s-' water velocity); salmonids were mostly in pools and runs, with 0. tshawytscha occupying slightly slower (0.1-0.2 m . s-') and deeper (20.5 m) waters than those occupied by S. trutta (0.2-0.5 m . SKIwater velocity and 10.4 m deep). The interspecific differences in feeding and microdistribution presumably reduce interaction between sympatric populations of galaxiids and salmonids.

## Field sampling

Deep Creek is a tributary of the upper Rangitata River, a major, cobble-bedded, braided river flowing to the east coast of the South Island (Fig. 1). The flow ( 3 4 m3.s-') in Deep Creek is primarily of groundwater origin from within the Rangitata River floodplain. The creek flows through an area covered primarily in tussock grasses and some scrub growth, such as matagouri (Discaria toumatou) and gorse (Ulex europaeusj.

Samples of fish and drifting invertebrates were collected on 10-11 April 1990 at 1730 (pre-dusk), 2200, 0530 (pre-dawn), and 1000 New Zealand standard time (NZSTj. None of the samplingtimes in this 2-day period was replicated, as previous related studies (Glova & Sagar 1989, 1991; Glova et al. 1992) showed minimal variation in fish diets between replicates. The sampling times were chosen as those that best described the die1 feeding patterns of each of the 4 species of fish with a minimum of sampling, based on knowledge of the daily activities of galaxiids and salmonids in other studies (Glova & Sagar 1989, 1991; Glova et al. 1992).Sunset occurred at 1800 and sunrise at 0700. The night was calm with a clear sky and a full moon.

The study area was located in the middle reach of the stream, which comprised 2 channels separated by an extensive island area. Each stream channel was approximately 4 8 m wide with extensive areas of moderately shallow runs and riffles, and stable beds and banks composed primarily of gravels and small cobbles.

The fish were collected with a pulsed DC backpack electro-shocker, working in an upstream direction to avoid disturbing fish in areas still to be shocked. Fish were killed immediately with an overdose of 2-phenoxyethanol and later preserved in 4% formaldehyde. Laboratory procedures

Fish stomach contents were examined under a stereoscopic microscope. Recently eaten foods (appeared undigested and were intact) were separated and counted. Aquatics were identified to species when possible, whereas terrestrial and aerial animals were identified to order only. Any digested material in the stomach was retained in a separate category, although the proportions of the different taxa present were not determined. The recently eaten foods were considered to be representative of the fish’s diet, although some variation in digestion rates of the different taxa was likely. Inclusion of biomass information on digested material was useful in interpreting die1 pattern of feeding by fish. Head capsule widths of invertebrates in the benthos, drift and diets of fish, of those taxa occurring in sufficient numbers for statistical analysis, were measured to the nearest 0.05 mm. All fish (including stomachs) and foods were dried separately at 70°C for 24 h and then weighed to the nearest 0.1 mg.

Differences in size-frequency distributions of the more common prey items in the diets of fish compared with those in the benthos and drift were tested by the Kolmogorov-Smirnov 2-sample test (Siegel 1956).

Frequency distributions of water velocities and water depths utilized by each fish species were computed, and 3-point moving averages of these data were used in plotting patterns of habitat use. Differences in frequency distributions of water depths and water velocities utilized by the fish were tested by the Kolmogorov-Smirnov two-sample test (Siegel 1956).

In contrast to the extensive ecological overlap between galaxiids and salmonids in our studies, Townsend & Crowl (1991) found a lack of cooccurrence (9 of 198 sites sampled) of G. vulgaris and S. trutta in 8 tributaries of a large, South Island, New Zealand river. Two factors that might account for the differences in results between these studies are the differences in streambed characteristics and the size of trout present. The streams sampled by Townsend & Crowl were dominated by sand and gravel beds, and the trout present were primarily of 1- and 2-year-old fish. In contrast, the beds in our streams were almost entirely of gravels and cobbles and the trout populations present were virtually of underyearling fish. There is some evidence that young galaxiids are vulnerable to predation by yearling brown trout, although less so when bottom cover (cobbles and rocks) is provided (Glova 1990). The lack of suitable bottom cover and a preponderance of yearling and older brown trout may be factors that contributed to the paucity of G. vulgaris co-occurring with brown trout in the streams studied by Townsend & Crowl (1991).

Although there was extensive overlap in resource use among populations of these 2 families of fishes in the present study and others (Glova & Sagar 1991; Glova et al. 1992), some interspecific differences existed. Typically. galaxiids foraged primarily from dusk to dawn, whereas salmonids foraged more from the pre-dawn to post-dusk period than in the middle of the night. Moreover, galaxiids frequently occupied shallower and faster waters than did salmonids. In addition, some differences existed in the diets of galaxiid populations in streams with and without salmonids present. Galaxiids in streams with salmonids present (present study; Glova & Sagar 1991; Glova et al. 1992) had proportionally less food in their stomachs, consumed virtually no terrestrial and aerial prey, ate a smaller size spectrum of prey and fed more during the day than did a G. idgaris population in a stream with no salmonids present (Glova & Sagar 1989). These inter-population differences in galaxiid feeding may be partly related to the effects of interaction with juvenile salmonids. Salmonids are known to be highly aggressive and territorial (Kalleberg 1958; Hartman 1965; Stein, Hall & Reimers 1972) and forage to quite an extent at night, as well as throughout the day, on larger prey (Sagar & Glova 1988, Glova et al. 1992). ## Discussion

Our findings on the interaction between galaxiids and juvenile salmonids in this study are consistent with those of several other studies of galaxiid/ salmonid communities in small New Zealand streams (Cadwallader 1975; Glova & Sagar 1991; Glova et al. 1992). The generalization that can be drawn from these works is that mixed populations of galaxiids and juvenile salmonids in gravel/cobble bedded streams are likely to overlap considerably in their use of food and space. However, the degree of overlap is quite variable and appears to be influenced by the number of species present.
