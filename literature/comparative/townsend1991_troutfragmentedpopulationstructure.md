# Fragmented population structure in a native New Zealand fish: an effect of introduced brown trout?

Colin R. Townsend and Todd A. Crowl

_Oikos_ 61(3), September 1991, pp. 347-354.

Stable URL: https://www.jstor.org/stable/3545242

We conducted a survey of 198 sites in eight catchments in the Taieri River drainage of

the South Island, New Zealand, and found a strong negative association between the distributions of introduced brown trout and native Galaxias vulgaris. Although habitat degradation due to agricultural and forestry practices was associated with

lower densities of fish, these reductions were found in all fish species and did not help

to explain the trout/galaxiid pattern. Statistical analyses incorporating a variety of

physical, chemical and biological variables showed that presence and abundance of

G. vulgaris were best predicted by the absence of trout. In most cases, G. vulgaris

were only found above waterfalls that were large enough to inhibit trout migration.

We suggest that predation is the most likely mechanism for the observed disjunct

distributions between the native fish and trout. Moreover, the strong predation effect

is probably a result of New Zealand's comparative paucity of native piscivorous

fishes, resulting in a native fish fauna having no evolved defenses.

## Discussion

A number of researchers in New Zealand and Australia

have suggested that abundances of galaxiid species have decreased and that their distributions have become frag-

mented (Allen 1961, McDowall 1968, 1987, Tilzey 1976,

Jackson and Williams 1980). Many have argued that the

introduction of brown trout has been a major cause in

observed declines, but it has also been suggested that habitat degradation was responsible (Weatherley and

Lake 1967, Lake 1971, Main 1988, Hayes et al. 1989,

Hanchet 1990, McDowall 1990). Because the introduc-

tion of brown trout and habitat degradation are both

highly correlated with human population density, it is

usually impossible to distinguish between the two fac-

tors. By incorporating a balanced study design across catchments of differing land use and, hence of differing

habitat degradation, we have been able to determine

whether G. vulgaris distribution is affected by intro-

duced trout, land use, or both.

Discriminant functions analysis has indicated that geomorphological variables primarily distinguish between the observed fish assemblages. Of these, the oc-

currence of waterfalls, at least 3 m high, downstream of

a sampling site has been shown to be the best predictor

of G. vulgaris occurrence. Because waterfalls of this size

are known to be barriers for upstream trout migration

(Stewart 1962), this finding can be interpreted in terms

of a negative impact by trout. If trout can inhabitat an
