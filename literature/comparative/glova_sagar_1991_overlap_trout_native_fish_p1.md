# Dietary and Spatial Overlap between Stream Populations of a Native and Two Introduced Fish Species in New Zealand

G. J. Glova and P. M. Sagar

Freshwater Fisheries Centre, Ministry of Agriculture and Fisheries, PO Box 8324, Riccarton, Christchurch, New Zealand.

## Abstract

Benthic and drifting invertebrates as well as fish were sampled in a lake-inlet stream over a 24-h period in summer in order to investigate interactions for food and space between a native fish species (Galaxias brevipinnis, Galaxiidae) and juveniles of two exotic fish species (Salmo trutta and Oncorhynchus mykiss, Salmonidae) in New Zealand. Galaxias brevipinnis fed almost exclusively at night on the benthos, whereas both trout species fed mostly during the day on drifting and benthic prey. Larvae of Deleatidium (Ephemeroptera), Hydrobiosis and Oxyethira (Trichoptera), and Austrosimulium and Chironomidae (Diptera) were the major prey in the diets of all three fish species. Small prey made up a greater proportion of the diet of G. brevipinnis than for either trout species. All three species of fish were found primarily in riffles and runs in water depths of 0.1-0.3 rn, with G. brevipinnis frequently being in faster-flowing areas (water velocity 0.8-1.1 m s-I) than was either trout species (water velocity 0.4-0.7 rn s-I). These differences in pattern of die1 feeding and microhabitat use no doubt lessen the interaction between the galaxiid and salrnonid fish species.

## Introduction

Because of its relatively depauperate native fish fauna, New Zealand has been the recipient of numerous fish introductions, primarily from Europe and North America late in the 1800s (McDowall 1985). Of the fish species introduced to this country, brown trout (Salmo trutta) and rainbow trout (Oncorhynchus mykiss) have been by far the most successful (McDowall 1990a). The distribution of these species is widespread and extensively overlaps that of native fish (McDowall 1990b). Competitive interaction between these salmonids and some of the native fishes, particularly those of the family Galaxiidae, is likely because of similarities in their body morphology and their ecology (McDowall 1968).

To date, assessment of the impact of exotic fishes on native fish stocks in New Zealand has been largely speculative (Allen 1961; McDowall 1968, 1979, 1985, 1990a). There is virtually no information on the status of the native fish stocks before the introduction of the various exotic fish species, and the evidence of impacts thus far has been largely circumstantial. Although competition, predation and displacement are likely mechanisms of impact of exotic fishes on native fish stocks, none of these mechanisms has been rigorously investigated in New Zealand.

The present study was aimed at documenting interaction for food and space between cohabiting stream populations of Galaxias brevipinnis and juveniles of brown and rainbow trout. Three hypotheses, based primarily on earlier observations of fish feeding and microhabitat preferences in a stream simulator with a natural food supply (Glova 1986), were tested: (1) temporally, feeding by G. brevipinnis overlaps little with that of either trout species; (2) the compositions of the diets of these three fish species overlap extensively; and (3) spatially, the distribution of G. brevipinnis overlaps considerably with that of both trout species in streams.

The study was carried out in the lower reach of the Ryton River, a lake-inlet stream in the South Island of New Zealand that had relatively abundant populations of these three fish species. During this study, the flow of the river was 1.2m3 s-' and water temperatures ranged from 13.0 to 20~2°C.The catchment and study area are described in an earlier related work (Glova and Sagar 1989a).

Materials and Methods

Field Sampling

Samples of fish and drifting invertebrates were collected at 1200, 2000, 2330, 0300 and 1200 hours (NZST) during 13-14 January 1987. The earliest and latest sets of samples in the series provided replication of results to assess variability in the diets of fish and in drifting invertebrates. Sampling times were chosen to coincide with the periods when feeding by fish was likely to start and end and to provide an indication of the intensity of feeding for each of the fish species. Sunset occurred at 2030 hours and sunrise at 0400 hours.

Fish were collected primarily in run and riffle habitats with the aid of pulsed d.c. backpack electroshocker. Sampling always progressed in an upstream direction to avoid disturbing fish for subsequent collections. Fish were immediately killed with an overdose of benzocaine and later preserved in 4% formaldehyde. The sample size of each species ranged from 28 to 30 fish for each of the five sets of samples, and fork lengths ranged from 45 to 101 mm for Galaxias brevipinnis, from 47 to 73 mm for Salmo trutta, and from 44 to 89 mm for Oncorhynchus mykiss.

Information on fish habitat use was gathered immediately upstream of the sites used for the earliest and second latest samplings. The procedure involved random sampling of 2-m2 segments of the stream with the backpack electroshocker and a fine-mesh stop net positioned at the downstream end of the segments. Water depth and water velocity (at 0.6 of water depth) were measured at the upstream, centre and downstream areas of each segment. The percentages of different substrate types present in each segment were estimated by eye. The fish caught in each segment were anaesthetized in a mild dose of 2-phenoxyethanol and then identified as to species, measured and released downstream.

Drifting-invertebrate samples were collected approximately 100m upstream of the latest (uppermost) fish-sampling site. Three random replicates of each of the five sets of drift samples were collected with 400-pm-mesh nets fitted to plastic samplers as described by Field-Dodgson (1985). Three random replicate benthic samples were collected during the day and at night immediately downstream of the drift-sampling site by means of a Waters and Knapp (1961) sampler fitted with a 350-pm-mesh net. All macroinvertebrate samples were preserved in 1.6% formaldehyde.

Laboratory Procedures

The stomach of each fish was dissected out for examination of its food contents under a stereoscopic microscope. Recently eaten foods (which appeared undigested and were intact) were separated and counted as Deleatidium nymphs, chironomid larvae, other aquatics, and terrestrial/aerial prey; the last two categories were identified as to species when possible. Digested remains were retained in a separate category, and no attempt was made to determine the proportions of different taxa present in them. Although some variation in digestion rates of different taxa was likely, the recently eaten foods were none the less considered to be representative of each fish's diet and were treated quantitatively. Head-capsule widths of invertebrates in the benthos, drift, and diet of fish that occurred in sufficient numbers for statistical analysis were measured to the nearest 0.05 mm. All fish (including stomachs) and foods (including digested remains) were dried separately at 70°C for 24 h and then weighed to the nearest 0.1 mg. Inclusion of information on digested remains was useful in interpreting the die1 pattern of feeding by fish.

## Benthos, Drift and Diets of Fish

Approximately 85% of the benthos by number in the Ryton River in summer was composed of the oligochaeteLumbriculus variegatus,Deleatidiurn (Ephemeroptera) nymphs, and larvae of Aoteapsyche (Trichoptera) and of Austrosimulium and Chironomidae (Diptera). Abundance and biomass of drifting invertebrates in this river were greatest in the period before midnight. Numerically, the drift was dominated by larvae of Oxyethira (Trichoptera), Austrosimulium and Chironomidae, pupae of Chironomidae, and adult Diptera and Trichoptera. Gravimetrically, the dominant organisms in the drift included Deleatidium nymphs, various larval Trichoptera, and adult Trichoptera and Diptera. A more detailed account of the macroinvertebrate fauna in the Ryton River is given by Glova and Sagar (1989~).

Aquatic invertebrates, principally Ephemeroptera, Trichoptera and Diptera, accounted for a major proportion of the food in the stomachs of the three species of fish (Table 1). Numerically, the diets of all three fish species were very similar, with Deleatidium nymphs and larvae of Oxyethira, Austrosimulium and Chironomidae comprising 88 to 95% of the prey eaten. Gravimetrically, however, some differences in diets between fish species existed, the more outstanding being that G. brevipinnis had greater proportions of Hydrobiosis umbripennis (Trichoptera) and Muscidae and a lower proportion of Oxyethira larvae than did both trout species. - be significantly correlated (Table 2). Although the compositions of the diets overlapped significantly on a numerical basis, overlap was less on a gravimetric basis and was considered nonsignificant between the galaxiid and the two trout species (Table 3).

## Prey Selection

All three species of fish fed selectively to some extent (Table 4), with chironomid larvae being the most preferred prey. Oxyethira albiceps larvae were also taken preferentially by these fishes but to a much lesser extent than chironomids. Some avoidance of Deleatidium nymphs and larvae of Aoteapsyche and Austrosimulium in the benthos appears to have occurred for all three fish species. Both trout species appear to have fed selectively on Deleatidium nymphs in the drift but strongly avoided chironomid pupae. There was little evidence for drift feeding by G. brevipinnis, based on differences in the percentage composition of prey in the drift versus those in the diet (data not included). ### Discussion

The findings of this study support the hypotheses tested. Galaxias brevipinnis, Salmo trutta and Oncorhynchus mykiss in the Ryton River overlapped considerably in their use of food and space, but they did so largely at different times of the day. All three species of fish ate mostly larval Deleatidium, Hydrobiosis, Oxyethria, Austrosimulium and Chironomidae, and they occupied relatively fast-flowing areas. Similarly, larval Ephemeroptera, Trichoptera and/or Diptera have been found to be important in the diets of other populations of galaxiids (Hopkins 1971; Bonnett et al. 1989; Glova and Sagar 1989b) and trout (Phillips 1929; Lane 1964; Hopkins 1970; McLennan and MacMillan 1984) in New Zealand streams.

Because the species of fish considered in the present study evolved in isolation from one another and make similar demands of their environment, they are potential competitors for

resources. In the Ryton River, however, temporal separation in feeding activity between G. brevipinnis and the two trout species probably minimizes competition. Overlap in feeding activity between these two families of fishes was largely restricted to the immediate postdusk period, when drift was found to be most abundant and benthic invertebrates are more likely to be out on the tops of stones (Pierce 1986), making them more readily available to fish.

The narrower dietary breadth of G. brevipinnis in the present study, as opposed to the more diverse diet of the species elsewhere (Rounick and Hicks 1985; Main and Winterbourn 1987), may indicate that their feeding in the Ryton River was restricted to a degree by interaction with trout. Both trout species fed, to some extent, in the early hours of the night and may have interacted with the galaxiid species. Juvenile salmonids have been observed to be behaviourally dominant over galaxiid fishes in a simulated stream environment (Glova 1989). Moreover, G. brevipinnis has been shown to be a flexible feeder of benthic and drifting prey (Main and Winterbourn 1987), and its close relative, G. vulgaris, has been reported to feed primarily on drifting prey in the immediate postdusk period (Glova and Sagar 1989b). In the present study, G. brevipinnis fed primarily on small benthic prey, whereas both trout species fed, to quite an extent, on the larger size fraction of both drifting and benthic prey. That drifting prey comprises a major proportion of the diet of these two salmonid species has been demonstrated by Elliott (1973). The ability of G. brevipinnis to feed on small benthic prey at night may be an advantage when this species is living with drift-feeding fish such as juvenile salmonids.

Extensive overlap in diet and habitat use is not just restricted to cohabiting populations of some of the native and exotic fish species in New Zealand, but occurs among some of the native fish species as well. As examples, torrentfish (Cheimarrichthys fosteri) and bluegilled bully (Gobiomorphus hubbsi) overlap extensively in diet (Scrimgeour and Winterbourn 1987) and habitat use (Glova and Duncan 1985). Similarly, the diets of two alpine galaxiids, Galaxias paucispondylus and G. prognathus, have been reported to overlap extensively (Bonnett et al. 1989). It is our view that since braided rivers are unstable channels (Mosley 1983) and the fish in them most commonly live in riffles (Glova 1988), a considerable degree of interspecific overlap in resource use among the fish inhabitants is largely unavoidable. In nonbraided channels, however, overlap in the use of food and space between galaxiids and juvenile salmonids may be substantially less, a possibility that will be investigated in future work.

In spite of the great amount of overlap in resource use among the galaxiid and trout populations in the Ryton River, there was no apparent evidence of adverse effects on them. The population density of the three species combined was 2.5 fish m-2, with G. brevipinnis and 0. mykiss each contributing 20% of the total fish numbers. These figures are well within the range of density estimates reported for fish populations in other braided rivers (Glova et al. 1985). Interactive effects among the three fish species in the present study were no doubt minimized by the fact that both trout populations were comprised primarily of fish less than 1 year old (as the older fish move into the lake) and by differences in their diel feeding patterns. Although these two families of fishes occupied similar habitats and ate similar foods, direct confrontation for food was most probably limited to twilight conditions. However, this statement must be tempered by the fact that these fish populations were sampled in a single 24-h period, and some variation in diel feeding patterns from those reported is possible over a longer time period.

In conclusion, it may well be that galaxiid fish stocks generally are not seriously affected by competition for food and space with salmonid fishes but rather by predation, as was speculated by Allen (1961). Glova (1990) demonstrated experimentally that galaxiid fry are highly vulnerable to predation by trout as a result of their free-swimming habit in the water column. Although no fish were found in the stomachs of trout in the present study, fish have been reported (Lane 1964) to be important in the diet of adult brown trout elsewhere. However, as the Ryton River populations of trout were comprised almost entirely of fish
