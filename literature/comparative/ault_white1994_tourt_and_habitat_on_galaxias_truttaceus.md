# Effects of Habitat Structure and the Presence of Brown Trout on the Population Density of Galaxias truttaceus in Tasmania, Australia

T. R. Ault and W. G. R. White

Department of Zoology, University of Tasmania, General Post Office Box 252C, Hobart, Tasmania 7001, Australia

Source: *Transactions of the American Fisheries Society* 123:939-949 (1994)

## Abstract

To examine the effect of introduced brown trout *Salmo trutta* on populations of native *Galaxias truttaceus* (Galaxiidae), known locally as spotted galaxias, the authors used population-abundance models based on habitat-use patterns of *G. truttaceus* to compare streams with and without brown trout. In selected streams in southeast Tasmania, habitat use by *G. truttaceus* was examined with respect to four principal components extracted from eight habitat variables.

Different size-classes of *G. truttaceus* displayed nonrandom habitat-use patterns, shifting from shallow, open habitats to deep, cover-rich habitats with increasing size. All size-classes preferred slow-flowing sections to fast-flowing sections. Population-abundance models were constructed for three size-classes of *G. truttaceus* and, despite the hydrological variability of the streams studied, all were reasonably successful in explaining variation.

When those models were applied to streams containing brown trout, the presence of brown trout was more important than habitat characteristics in determining the abundance of *G. truttaceus*. In streams with brown trout, the density of each size-class was substantially lower than expected on the basis of habitat alone. The study therefore provides strong evidence that brown trout adversely affect populations of *G. truttaceus*, because habitat differences were quantitatively accounted for when streams with and without brown trout were compared.

## Introduction

The southern-hemisphere family Galaxiidae and the northern-hemisphere family Salmonidae are thought to share a common phylogenetic origin, probably in Pangaea during the Mesozoic (McDowall 1969, 1990; Fink 1984). Since the breakup of Pangaea, however, the two groups evolved in isolation and only recently became reassociated through anthropogenic introductions (McDowall 1990). The introduction of salmonids, primarily brown trout *Salmo trutta*, to southern-hemisphere streams has caused particular concern because of their biological and ecological similarity to galaxiids. Many authors have attributed declines in galaxiid stocks to introduced salmonids (McDowall 1968, 1990; Tilzey 1976; Cadwallader 1979; Minns 1990), although much of the evidence has been circumstantial.

Brown trout were introduced into Tasmania in 1864 and were soon stocked into most catchments to provide angling opportunities. Almost nothing is known about the native freshwater fish fauna before that date, so it is difficult to assess the full impact of the introduction of brown trout. In addition, land use has changed considerably over time, and there has long been debate about whether declines in native fish stocks are attributable mainly to habitat alteration or to introductions of exotics (McDowall 1968; Tilzey 1976; Cadwallader 1979; Minns 1990; Townsend and Crowl 1991). In southeast Tasmania, *Galaxias truttaceus*, commonly known as spotted galaxias, is the numerically dominant fish species in undisturbed streams. In streams containing introduced brown trout, however, *G. truttaceus* is either absent or present at very low densities.

The aim of the study was to examine interactions between brown trout and *G. truttaceus* and to determine whether the presence of brown trout is responsible for declines in *G. truttaceus* populations. Two approaches were adopted. First, a quantitative analysis of habitat use by *G. truttaceus* allowed comparison with previous descriptions of habitat use by brown trout and an assessment of potential niche overlap. Second, habitat preferences were used to develop population-abundance models based on habitat characteristics. Those models were then used to separate the effect of habitat differences from the effect of brown trout when differences in galaxiid density among streams and sites were examined.

## Methods

Field studies were conducted from May to September 1991. Habitat use by *G. truttaceus* was assessed at 35 sites in five southeast Tasmanian streams lacking brown trout, and those data were used to construct population-abundance models. Additional comparison sites were examined in streams containing brown trout.

At each site, 5-mm-mesh blocking nets were placed at upstream and downstream boundaries, enclosed fish were removed by electroshocking, and fish were measured, anaesthetised, and then returned to the stream. Sites were selected to represent relatively homogeneous macrohabitats such as pools, riffles, and runs, and were chosen with reference to accessibility, depth, water velocity, and substrate composition.

For each site, stream-section length and width were measured, and cross-stream transects were used to record depth, velocity, substrate, and cover. Habitat description was ultimately reduced to eight variables, including mean depth, mean velocity, mean substrate particle size, proportions of silt plus algae, detritus, macrophytic vegetation, and proportions of overhead and instream cover.

Most variables were transformed before analysis. A principal component analysis reduced the habitat data to a smaller set of descriptor axes, and fish were divided into three size-classes. Nonrandom habitat use was tested along the principal-component axes, and standard multiple regression was then used to model the abundance of each size-class in brown-trout-free streams. Those regression models were applied to streams containing brown trout to evaluate whether observed densities differed from habitat-based expectations.

## Results

The first four principal-component axes explained 80.4% of the variance in the original eight habitat variables. All three size-classes of *G. truttaceus* showed nonrandom habitat use. Medium and large fish were strongly overrepresented in low-velocity, silty habitats and in deeper habitats with overhead cover, whereas small fish showed a different pattern and were less clearly associated with deep covered habitats.

Regression models for small, medium, and large *G. truttaceus* explained approximately 54%, 52%, and 43% of the variation in fish density, respectively. When those models were applied to streams containing brown trout, observed densities of *G. truttaceus* were consistently lower than expected, supporting the inference that brown trout depress native galaxiid abundance beyond what can be explained by habitat differences alone.

## Discussion

The recovered discussion supports the interpretation that habitat use differs by size class, with medium and large *G. truttaceus* concentrating in deeper, more covered, lower-velocity habitats and smaller fish using somewhat different habitat combinations. These patterns are consistent with earlier qualitative accounts of the species and with the general locomotory constraints of mid-water salmoniform fishes in streams.

The modelling results strengthen the case that brown trout negatively affect *G. truttaceus* because habitat differences were explicitly accounted for before densities were compared. The author also places these findings in the broader Australasian literature on galaxiids and introduced salmonids, arguing that the most likely mechanisms are a mixture of interspecific competition and predation.
