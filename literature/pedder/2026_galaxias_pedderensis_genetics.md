# Evaluating genetic diversity, population structure, and introgression in insurance populations of one of Australia’s most threatened species: the freshwater fish Galaxias pedderensis

Kayleigh Horner1 · Charlotte Jense1 · Elize Y. X. Ng1 · Rob Freeman2 · Bruce Deagle3 · Christopher P. Burridge1

Received: 31 March 2025 / Accepted: 19 October 2025 © The Author(s), under exclusive licence to Springer Nature B.V. 2025

## Abstract

Anthropogenic impacts threaten biodiversity, sometimes necessitating the establishment of insurance populations through translocation. Ideally such populations provide a robust means to maintain a threatened species and its genetic diversity. This study investigates the genetic diversity and integrity of translocated populations of the freshwater fish, Pedder galaxias (Galaxias pedderensis), and contrasts their genetic diversity with related species. Galaxias pedderensis was once abundant in Lake Pedder, Tasmania, but declined following the artificial raising of water levels for hydroelectricity generation in 1972. This resulted in habitat modification and the invasion of brown trout (Salmo trutta) and climbing galaxias (Galaxias brevipinnis), both considered threats to other galaxiid fishes through predation and competition. In 1991, prior to G. pedderensis extirpation in Lake Pedder, a translocated population was established based on 45 individuals, then in 2001 a second (serial) translocated population was established from the first. We examined genetic diversity in both translocated populations using double digest restriction-site associated DNA sequencing. The two G. pedderensis populations had similar levels of heterozygosity and low genetic differentiation, indicating that there is desirable replication of standing genetic variation. Galaxias pedderensis showed lower heterozygosity than the widespread and common G. brevipinnis, but higher heterozygosity than swan galaxias (Galaxias fontanus), another translocated endangered species, potentially reflecting differences in the duration of their low abundances. We also tested and found evidence of introgression from G. brevipinnis into G. pedderensis, but this may pre-date their anthropogenic contact. This research can inform future genetic management of G. pedderensis, including ongoing exchange and sourcing of individuals for any additional translocated populations.

Keywords: Serial translocation · Heterozygosity · Genetic rescue · Insurance population · Invasive species · Introgression

## Introduction

Biodiversity is increasingly threatened by anthropogenic impacts such as habitat alteration, invasive species, and climate change (Ceballos et al. 2015). In many instances species are becoming critically rare in situations where anthropogenic threats cannot be mitigated, such as urbanisation (Li et al. 2022). Management to conserve such species may therefore centre on the establishment of new populations, often in captivity—“insurance populations”or through translocation to areas where threats are diminished—“rescue populations” (McGowan et al. 2017; Morris et al. 2021; Gooley et al. 2022). Such populations should be managed to maximise genetic diversity captured and

retained (Shaw et al. 2025). This includes the construction of multiple new populations to avoid catastrophic loss associated with the decline of a single insurance or rescue population, and periodic movement of individuals among populations to reduce future loss of genetic diversity from what would otherwise be small, closed systems (Hedrick 1995; Mills and Allendorf 1996; Tallmon et al. 2004; Weeks et al. 2011; Gooley et al. 2022). However, sourcing sufficient individuals of threatened species to capture and maintain genetic diversity can be challenging. In some instances, there can also be concerns that the genetic representativeness of sourced individuals may have been compromised by introgression from more common, sympatric related species (Bohling 2016).

The Pedder galaxias, Galaxias pedderensis Frankenberg 1968 (Galaxiidae), is a small (75–120 mm fork length) fish previously endemic to Lake Pedder and adjacent streams in southwest Tasmania, Australia (Fig. 1). The species completes its lifecycle in freshwater (non-diadromous life history; McDowall 2006; Fig. 1) and was considered abundant during the 1960s when it was formally described (Frankenberg 1968; Andrews 1976). However, Lake Pedder was artificially raised in 1972, which led to two previously separate catchments becoming one and resulted in the invasion of brown trout (Salmo trutta) and the climbing galaxias (Galaxias brevipinnis). In addition, a one-off stocking of Lake Pedder was conducted using 350,000 hatchery-raised S. trutta fry in 1972 (Hamr 1992; Chilcott et al. 2013). Collectively, these introductions and the inundation of G. pedderensis habitat dramatically changed the ecology of the system (Hamr 1992; Chilcott et al. 2013). Galaxias pedderensis initially thrived, likely due to increased food availability commonly observed following the flooding of terrestrial habitat, and attained peak abundance in 1977 (Hamr 1992; Lake 1998, 2001; Chilcott et al. 2013). However, S. trutta were documented to consume G. pedderensis during the 1970s and 1980s (Sanger 2001), possibly explaining the large ‘trophy’ sized S. trutta present (French 2011). In general, S. trutta is considered a threat to galaxiids due to predation and competition (Hardie et al. 2006; McDowall 2006), although some galaxiid populations have co-existed with S. trutta for more than 100 years (Sanger and Fulton 1989; Fulton 1990). In the early 1980s G. pedderensis became exceptionally rare (Sanger 1988; Sanger and Fulton 1989; Lake 1998). Comprehensive surveys conducted during 1990–91 yielded less than 80 individuals from only 5 of 117 sites within the catchment, although the majority of fish were caught in just two streams (Hamr 1992). Between 1992 and 96 only 10 individuals were collected (Chilcott et al. 2013), at which point it was potentially the most threatened fish species in Australia (Hamr 1995). Surveys continued until 2002, but the last G. pedderensis was observed in the lake

during 1996; thus G. pedderensis has a formal conservation status of ‘Extinct in the wild’ as of 2005 under the Australian EPBC Act 1999 as it only occurs outside its natural range. G. pedderensis is also listed as ‘endangered’ under the Tasmanian TSP Act 1995 and the global IUCN Red List of Threatened Species.

While G. pedderensis was the dominant galaxiid in the system prior to 1980, in the early 1980s, approximately 10 years after the modification of Lake Pedder, the invasive G. brevipinnis became the most abundant galaxiid (Sanger 2001). By 1988 G. brevipinnis appeared abundant in streams draining into Lake Pedder (Sanger 1988), and given its interactions with other galaxiids elsewhere (Hamr 1992; McDowall and Allibone 1994) it likely competed with and preyed upon G. pedderensis. Galaxias brevipinnis has a higher fecundity and competitive advantage compared to other galaxiids, and has a greater ability to climb barriers than S. trutta, and hence could have contributed to the decline in G. pedderensis in areas inaccessible to S. trutta (McDowall 2006). Additionally, at low abundance, the remaining G. pedderensis may have hybridised with G. brevipinnis, given the two species’ relatively close phylogenetic relationship (Burridge et al. 2012; Adams et al. 2023) and observations of hybridisation in other galaxiids (Esa et al. 2000; Adams et al. 2014; Waters et al. 2023), thus threatening the genetic integrity of G. pedderensis.

Based on the observed decline in G. pedderensis and the inability to eradicate S. trutta and G. brevipinnis from Lake Pedder, ~45 individuals were translocated from Lake Pedder to the much smaller (16 ha) Lake Oberon (Fig. 1) between 1991 and 1997, with seven of these representing descendants of two females and 4 males from captive breeding trials (Sanger 2001; Chilcott et al. 2013). Furthermore, all individuals were derived from two streams draining into Bonnet Bay, Lake Pedder (Fig. 1). The Lake Oberon population grew sufficiently large to safely be used to establish a second population in a small water supply reservoir (1.7 ha) at Strathgordon (Fig. 1; Chilcott et al. 2013). This was derived from a total of 353 individuals from Lake Oberon: 52 in 2001, 22 in 2002, 117 in 2005, 53 in 2006 and 109 in 2007 (Chilcott et al. 2013). No recruitment was observed at Strathgordon until 2008, following improvement to potential spawning habitat, then by 2012 several hundred individuals across a range of size classes were evident (Chilcott et al. 2013). Three G. brevipinnis individuals were observed and removed from the Strathgordon reservoir (Chilcott et al. 2013), indicating that loss of genetic integrity due to hybridisation may also be a problem in this insurance population, but otherwise the Strathgordon reservoir is isolated from Lake Pedder by barriers.

Overall, our study seeks to quantify genetic variation and genetic integrity of G. pedderensis populations, which relates to a listed priority in the current management plan for this species to inform future management decisions to maintain genetic diversity (Department of Primary Industries and Water 2006).

### Materials and methods

Galaxias pedderensis were sampled from two isolated insurance populations (n=31; Fig. 1) by Inland Fisheries Tasmania and deposited in the Australian National Fish Collection. Galaxias brevipinnis (n=54) were sampled from rivers and lakes and included specimens of both diadromous and non-diadromous life histories. Half of the samples were collected from Lake Pedder and its catchment, representing the lineage that likely coexisted with G. pedderensis in the late 1900s. Galaxias fontanus were sampled from two translocated populations in north-eastern Tasmania (n=10). Samples comprised pelvic fin tissue preserved in ethanol at

- 4 °C or frozen at −80 °C. DNA was extracted using the Qiagen DNeasy Blood &

Tissue kit, following the spin column protocol for animal tissue. The Australian Genome Research Facility conducted double digest restriction-site associated DNA sequencing (dd-RAD-seq) using restriction enzymes PstI and MseI. Paired-end sequencing was performed on one 10B lane of an Illumina NovaSeq X Plus Sequencing System (Peterson et al. 2012).

The paired-end FASTQ files were checked for read quality using FastQC (Andrews 2010). Sequenced reads were then demultiplexed and reads that were of low quality and with uncalled bases were removed using the “process_radtags” program in Stacks version 2.67 (Catchen et al. 2013). During demultiplexing, barcodes and RAD_Tag cut sites were also rescued if required. Bases with a phred score<30

were trimmed using Trimmomatic version 0.39 (Bolger et al. 2014). Reads that did not meet the minimum length of 36 were excluded for further analysis. FastQC was then used again to check the trimmed read quality. Trimmed reads were then aligned using bwa.mem in Burrows-Wheeler Aligner (BWA) version 0.7.17 (Li and Durbin 2009; Li 2014) to the closest available reference genome, Galaxias fontanus (GenBank accession: GCA_043388395.1). The alignments were then filtered to keep those with mapping quality≥20

- (-q 20) and sorted by coordinate order using SAMtools version 1.16.1 (Li et al. 2009). We used a reference genomebased approach in Stacks version 2.67 (Catchen et al. 2013), aligning reads to the G. fontanus reference genome. SNPs and genotypes were called using the “gstacks” module, which assembles loci and performs variant calling based on the reference alignment.

Heterozygosity was calculated and compared between all populations of G. pedderensis, G. brevipinnis and G. fontanus. This was to contrast the relative genetic diversity between two non-diadromous species that are formally listed as endangered and sampled from translocated populations, and a species that is common and widespread, including both non-diadromous and diadromous life histories. Autosomal heterozygosity (across both monomorphic and polymorphic loci) was calculated to reduce bias in comparison of genetic diversity among populations and species (Schmidt et al. 2021). To determine the autosomal heterozygosity of each population, the “populations” program in Stacks was run and all loci were called for each population

- (-r 1). SNPs required a minimum of 10 reads per individual to be retained (--min-gt-depth 10). Autosomal heterozygosity was then obtained from the output summary statistics. Observed heterozygosity (polymorphic sites only) for each

population was also calculated. FIS, a measure of inbreeding in a population, was also calculated within “populations” (--smooth-popstats), with p-values constructed from 1,000 bootstrapping permutations to determine if FIS significantly deviated from zero (--bootstrap-pifis and --bootstrap-reps 1000).

All other analyses undertaken in this study, including observed heterozygosity, used genotypes from separate “populations” runs to call only binary SNPs. Only the first SNP per locus was called (--write-single-snp), with a minimum of 10 reads per individual, and a minimum of 90% of individuals in a population to process a locus (-r 0.9). PLINK version 1.90b6.26 (Purcell et al. 2007) was used to filter for linkage disequilibrium using --indep-pairwise 200 50 0.5 (Knaus and Grünwald 2017). The filtered SNP data was converted to variant call format (VCF) for further analysis. For analyses of only G. pedderensis samples, “gstacks” and “populations” were run with the same parameters and filtered for linkage disequilibrium.

Population structuring was assessed for the filtered binary SNP data with ADMIXURE version 1.3.0 (Alexander et al. 2009; Alexander and Lange 2011). ADMIXTURE uses a maximum likelihood framework to estimate the coancestries of individuals with SNP data. It was run with K—representing the hypothesised number of populations of origin—ranging from 2 to 13 (the number of geographic sources). Cross-validation was used to choose results based on K. ADMIXURE results were visualised with the ggplot2 package in R version 4.4.1 (R_Core_Team 2024). To test for significant differences in allele frequencies between G. pedderensis populations, we performed an Analysis of Molecular Variance (AMOVA; Excoffier et al. 1992) using the poppr version 2.9.6 package in R (Kamvar et al. 2014). The analysis was implemented with 999 Monte-Carlo permutations.

Introgressive hybridisation between G. pedderensis and G. brevipinnis was tested using a D-statistics approach (Durand et al. 2011; Patterson et al. 2012), implemented through Dtrios within Dsuite version 0.5 - r57 (Malinsky et al. 2021). This is also known as the “ABBA-BABA” test, in which a phylogenetic topology {[(P1, P2), (P3)], O}, where P1, P2 and P3 indicate the position of the species in the phylogenetic tree (e.g., see Supplementary Fig. 1), is provided for testing introgression between either P1 or P2 with P3 based on the frequency of “ABBA” (P2 with P3) and “BABA” (P1 with P3) patterns. We tested all possible pairs of G. brevipinnis populations at P1 and P2 with either Lake Oberon or Strathgordon G. pedderensis at P3 (72 trios). Likewise, we tested both G. pedderensis populations at P1 and P2 with each possible G. brevipinnis population at P3 (9 trios). Galaxias brevipinnis populations of prime interest were from Lake Pedder and its connected creeks: Pebbly Creek and Sentinel Creek. All tests used G. fontanus as the outgroup. Statistical significance was based on a D-statistic unequal to zero, using the Benjamini and Hochberg (2018) procedure for false discovery rate during simulation tests.

Coalescent simulations were also used to test for the presence and timing of introgression between G. pedderensis and G. brevipinnis, which could be interpreted relative to the 1972 inundation of Lake Pedder. Fastsimcoal2 (Excoffier et al. 2021) was used to infer the pattern of introgression. Five models of gene flow were simulated: (A) species divergence without introgression, (B) divergence with constant introgression, (C) introgression only during initial divergence, and (D) divergence with delayed onset introgression, either unconstrained in time or (D’) with introgression only within the last 50 generations (reflecting invasion of Lake Pedder by G. brevipinnis; Supplementary Fig. 2). The site frequency spectrum (SFS) was converted from the VCF using EasySFS (Gutenkunst et al. 2009). For each simulated model, the SFS was fitted to the empirical data while maximising the composite-likelihood as recommended

(Excoffier et al. 2021). A wide range of initial starting parameters were allowed and randomly selected. Parameters were subsequently optimised using 50 runs of 500,000 coalescent simulations and the best model was determined based on maximum likelihood and the Akaike information criterion (AIC). Confidence intervals for the best model were estimated through bootstrapping. To account for stochasticity in likelihood estimation, we also performed 100 independent fastsimcoal2 runs that were initialised with parameter values from the best-supported model. To infer the results in absolute time, we invoked a generation length of 3 years based on the average age of reproduction for G. pedderensis and G. brevipinnis (Hamr 1992; Rowe et al. 2002; Department of Primary Industries and Water 2006; Department of Natural Resources and Environment Tasmania 2023), and mutation rate of 1.0×10 8 per generation (Spivakov et al. 2014; Feng et al. 2017; Stobie et al. 2018; Kim et al. 2019; Bergeron et al. 2022, 2023; Zhang et al. 2023).

### Results

A total of 2,958,806,166 paired-end 150 bp Illumina sequence reads were obtained from 95 individuals. After processing for sequencing quality, 2,027,720,428 (68.53%) reads were retained with a mean of 21,344,425 reads per individual. 311,499 SNPs were then called for autosomal heterozygosity analysis (retaining all polymorphic and monomorphic sites). For further downstream analyses, and after filtering for linkage, 11,784 binary SNPs were called across all three species, while 9,252 SNPs were called for G. pedderensis samples alone. Population autosomal heterozygosities and observed heterozygosities of G. pedderensis were lower than in G. brevipinnis, but higher than in G. fontanus (Fig. 2). The inbreeding coefficient, FIS, did not deviate from zero in either G. pedderensis population (p>0.999).

For the entire dataset, ADMIXTURE favoured K=4, with K=3 closely behind, as they exhibited the lowest cross-validation errors of Δ 0.000 and Δ 0.001, respectively. Both K=3 and K=4 supported genetic distinctiveness of all species, with K=4 showing additional structuring within G. brevipinnis (Fig. 3; Supplementary Fig. 3). The ADMIXTURE plot of the two G. pedderensis populations with K=2 showed no clear evidence of population structuring (Supplementary Fig. 4). AMOVA indicated that the molecular variation within the two G. pedderensis populations was far greater than the variation between populations, indicating very low population genetic differentiation (Φ=0.011), but this was still significantly greater than zero (p=0.007).

### Discussion

#### Genetic diversity

The similar heterozygosities observed in both G. pedderensis populations (Lake Oberon and Strathgordon Dam), combined with their low genetic differentiation, represents a positive situation for these two translocated populations. Furthermore, there was no evidence of significant inbreeding in either population. While the genetic differentiation was statistically significant, this is consistent with the high statistical power provided by comparisons involving thousands of loci, and biological significance of this difference may be low (Waples et al. 2008). In essence, the genetic diversity captured in Lake Oberon is also represented in Strathgordon Dam, which indicates that the translocation involving 353 individuals sufficiently captured the genetic diversity of the Oberon population, despite serial translocations being not recommended (Mock et al. 2004). It would be unlikely that their similar heterozygosity is a product of both populations declining equally through time, as independent loss of genetic variation through drift would be expected to manifest as pronounced genetic differentiation. Our observation of equal genetic diversity and limited genetic differentiation among serially translocated populations is much more favourable than outcomes from other threatened species undergoing such management (Seymour et al. 2001; Lambert et al. 2005; Biebach and Keller 2009; Finger et al. 2013); the latter may reflect the use of

Given the lack of pre-decline samples suitable for the assessment of genetic diversity loss in G. pedderensis, we performed contrasts against another threatened galaxiid and a common galaxiid species. Significantly lower mean observed heterozygosity was observed in the translocated threatened G. pedderensis and G. fontanus compared to the more widespread, common, and often diadromous G. brevipinnis, but this is consistent with expectations based on life history (Allibone and Wallis 1993; McDowall 2001; Delgado et al. 2020). The significantly higher population heterozygosities in G. pedderensis than G. fontanus may reflect, among other things, differences in their population declines. The G. pedderensis decline was potentially from a larger population (a lake) and was relatively rapid (~20 years or less) and was followed by fast growth in the translocated populations, which is desirable for the retention of genetic variation (Weeks et al. 2011; Frankham et al. 2014). Contrarily, G. fontanus has existed in small, fragmented populations in headwater streams since its discovery in 1978, possibly due to the natural history of the species, but also potentially due to the established presence of S. trutta at that time (Fulton 1978), with which G. fontanus cannot coexist (Department of Primary Industries and Water 2006). The number of individuals translocated for G. fontanus, ~50 from the same source in 1993 (Crook and Sanger 1998; Department of Primary Industries and Water 2006), is similar to the original G. pedderensis translocation

(n=45). Consequently, this is unlikely to explain the present species differences in heterozygosity. We next assess the genetic integrity of G. pedderensis given secondary contact with G. brevipinnis, and this may have also contributed to the elevated genetic diversity in G. pedderensis relative to G. fontanus, as has been observed for the threatened Iberian lynx, Lynx pardinus (Lucena-Perez et al. 2024).

### Genetic integrity

One of our aims was to assess if the genetic integrity of G. pedderensis could have been compromised by introgression from G. brevipinnis following its invasion into Lake Pedder after 1972. As the ABBA-BABA test showed introgression with G. brevipinnis from the Lake Pedder region as well as from non-diadromous populations elsewhere, it is possible that (a) they hybridised in Lake Pedder or Strathgordon Dam after 1972 or (b) the ABBA-BABA results were an indication of earlier introgression. While the coalescent simulations could not reject the possibility that introgression followed the flooding of Lake Pedder, there is clear evidence that it did precede it (up to 1.4 Mya). While the ADMIXTURE plot also showed no evidence of introgression in the current populations, this signal can be lost quite quickly if there was only a short bout of introgression followed by a period in which G. brevipinnis-sourced alleles would become recombined and homogenised within G. pedderensis, producing a G. pedderensis population that appears uniformly distinct from G. brevipinnis (given a lack of known non-introgressed reference samples; Ravagni et al. 2021). This process will be faster in the smaller populations of threatened species and may explain why definitive examples for such taxa are rare despite a high frequency of introgressive hybridisation from taxa more broadly (Lucena-Perez et al. 2024).

### Conservation implications

Our study meets a key recommendation of the current G. pedderensis management plan to assess levels of genetic variation in the species (Department of Primary Industries and Water 2006). While there was minimal genetic differentiation of the two translocated G. pedderensis populations, their genetic trajectories will diverge through time by genetic drift at a rate proportional to their population sizes (Nei and Tajima 1981; Wares and Pringle 2008). To combat this, translocation of only one individual per generation between populations is required (Hedrick 1995; Mills and Allendorf 1996; Tallmon et al. 2004; Weeks et al. 2011; Gooley et al. 2022). However, such translocations also need

to be considered relative to risks such as disease transmission or inadvertent movement of other species.

The similar genetic variation in both populations has bearing on the establishment of an additional translocated population, which is to be considered according to the current management plan for G. pedderensis (Department of Primary Industries and Water 2006). Founding individuals could be sourced exclusively from the more logistically feasible Strathgordon population. However, it should be noted that the Strathgordon population is at greater risk of decline than Lake Oberon due to proximity with populations of brown trout and G. brevipinnis, and hence may not be suitable when needed. Establishing an additional insurance population, while desirable, is also challenged by environmental requirements, and the potential biodiversity consequences of introducing G. pedderensis by necessity into a system lacking in other predatory fish (Chilcott et al. 2013). Re-establishment of a viable population in Lake Pedder is not feasible given the challenge of eradicating S. trutta and G. brevipinnis (Chilcott et al. 2013).

While our inferences of introgression between G. pedderensis and G. brevipinnis following the inundation of Lake Pedder are equivocal, if it did occur it may be considered positive or negative depending on the conservation context of the species (Roberts et al. 2009; Vanhaecke et al. 2012; Todesco et al. 2016). For instance, introgression threatens the genetic novelty of species and hence can be considered a threatening process for biodiversity, with key examples involving introgression from domesticated canids and felids (Oliveira et al. 2008; Galaverni et al. 2017). However, avoiding introgression and preserving genetic purity may lead a species to extinction if low genetic variation is a key contributor to decline (Love Stowell et al. 2017; Burridge 2019); the apparently high fitness of both G. pedderensis populations, indicated by observations of rapid population growth, could be a positive outcome from recent introgression. Likewise, if the genetic diversity and fitness in the G. pedderensis populations were at a point of concern, genetic rescue using novel alleles from G. brevipinnis may be beneficial (Hogg et al. 2006; Frankham 2015; Whiteley et al. 2015; Burridge 2019; Johnson et al. 2021). Pre-emptively testing the ability to produce viable G. pedderensis

G. brevipinnis hybrids would be desirable to avoid having to do this at short notice—and whether this is even a feasible option—as a management intervention (Hogg et al. 2006; Burridge 2019). It may also be informative with respect to whether these species may have hybridised in the wild.
