# Threatened fishes of the world: Galaxias pedderensis Frankenberg, 1968 (Galaxiidae)

Premek Hamr. Canadian Wildlife Service, Canada Centre for Inland Waters, Box 5050 Burlington, Ontario L7R 4A6, Canada.

Common name: Pedder galaxias. Conservation status: Endangered (Threatened Fish Committee, Aust. Soc. Fish Biol. 1989)

Identification : D 9-13, A 9-14, P 11-14 . Vertebral count 49-55 (mean 51) Small, scaleless, freshwater fish, adult size range 75-

156 mm. Colouration: light yellow-brown with gold hue and blackish-brown irregular blotches dorsally and laterally, ventral surface off-white. Drawing by P. Hamr.

Distribution: Endemic to Tasmania, Australia . Restricted to small region of South-West Tasmania . Formerly found in Lakes Pedder and Maria, Serpentine River and associated small tributaries . Presently found in only two small tributaries entering Bonnet Bay in the Pedder impoundment . Abundance: Formerly very adundant throughout its original range and, following the flooding of the original lake and its watershed in 1972, in the new Pedder impoundment . Rapid decline in numbers occurred in the early 1980s and subsequently the species disappeared from the impoundment and most of its tributaries. Presently found in very low numbers in the two small tributaries of the Pedder impoundment. During the summer of1991-1992, a small population consisting of adult wild stock and laboratory reared juveniles has been translocated to a highland lake in the Western Arthur Range in South-West Tasmania. Habitat and ecology: Formerly found near edges of (original) Lake Pedder and lower portions of its tributaries . Presently, G. pedderensis is found in the lower meandering portions of both streams it inhabits, up to 2 km upstream from the impoundment . It is most abundant in forested sections where the stream is highly convoluted with deep pools, relatively slow flow, sandy bottom, and abundant cover . Diet consists mainly of terrestrial and aquatic arthropods (terrestrial insects, aquatic insect larvae, aquatic beetles, crayfish, copepods) . Reproduction: Spawning takes place in spring (mid October) when water temperatures first begin to rise (at approximately 7° C). Female G. pedderensis produce a relatively small number (200-1200) of large eggs (2.2 to2.5 mm in diameter) . In captivity, eggs were deposited under flat rocks as well as underneath aquatic vegetation and submerged driftwood. Size at maturity 95 mm FL in females and 76 mm FL in males . Adults can be sexed by external examination of genital papillae : in the male the papilla is narrow, while in the female it is broad and rounded. These external features can be distinguished through the year but are most pronounced during the reproductive season . Threats: Competition and predation from the exotic brown trout, Salmo trutta, and the native climbing galaxias, Galaxias brevipinnis, which have been introduced and/or have invaded the impoundment and its tributaries following the inundation of the original habitat . Destruction and loss of original low land/slow-flowing habitat due to flooding. Conservation action:All of the remaining habitat and populations (original and translocated) are found within the South-West National Park/World Heritage Area. Intensive surveys between 1989 and 1991established that only two small populations remained in the wild . Introduced species captured in these two remaining habitats were removed . The invasion and proliferation of exotics remains a major problem in these habitats however . A portion of the wild population was translocated to a highland lake which had suitable water quality and temperature regime as well as abundant food supply and suitable spawning habitat but was isolated enough to prevent invasion by trout or climbing galaxiids . Captive breeding and artificial fertilization of ova (in 1991) resulted in limited success with only 11 individuals raised through to juvenile period . These laboratory-raised fish were subsequently released into the translocated population . Conservation recommendations: Careful monitoring of existing wild and translocated fish should continue. Captive breeding should be continued to augment original and translocated stocks. Additional translocated population(s) should be established if sufficient stock becomes available . Rehabilitation of the two Bonnett Bay creeks may be attempted. This would entail continued removal (and possible exclusion) of introduced species as well as restocking with any surplus G. pedderensis resulting from captive breeding. Remarks: Because of its restricted distribution and the very low numbers of individuals remaining (apparently lower than 100 individuals), this species must now be considered as the most endangered vertebrate in Tasmania and perhaps as the freshwater fish species in the gravest danger of extinction in Australia.
