## Executive Summary

Executive summary of results and recommendations for project P100. The study examined the distributions, life histories, and environmental requirements of three endemic Tasmanian species of *Galaxias* and developed management recommendations from those findings.

### Galaxias fontanus

Several new populations of *G. fontanus* (Swan galaxias) were discovered, bringing the total known number to five. The new populations were all found in small tributaries of the Macquarie River, about 40 km south of the previously known Swan River population above Hardings Falls. All known populations remain threatened by brown trout, although natural barriers currently provide some protection.

The species is a small to medium-sized, spring-spawning galaxiid. It first spawns at about two years of age and is an opportunistic carnivore that can make substantial use of terrestrial food.

### Summary of management recommendations

- Conservation status should remain as endangered.
- Habitat protection measures should be actively promoted and their integrity maintained.
- Where possible, barriers to brown trout dispersal should be strengthened.
- Brown trout should be removed from streams containing *G. fontanus* where feasible.
- Breeding populations should be established by translocation to suitable streams.

### Galaxias johnstoni

Several new populations of *G. johnstoni* (Clarence galaxias) were discovered during the study, all confined to isolated sections of the Clarence River system. A significant new population was found on Wentworth Hills, about 20 km south of Clarence Lagoon. The species appears unable to tolerate brown trout competition and predation in riverine habitats, and is absent from sections of the Clarence system where brown trout are established.

The species appears able to coexist with brook trout (*Salvelinus fontinalis*) in Clarence Lagoon. It is a medium to large galaxiid that feeds heavily on aquatic crustaceans in lacustrine habitats. A spawning migration into an inflowing creek was observed in one population, and adhesive eggs were found beneath rocks in the stream. Egg masses suggested mass-spawning events.

### Summary of management recommendations

- Conservation status should remain as endangered.
- Habitat protection measures should be actively promoted.
- Inland Fisheries Commission stocking policy should recognise the vulnerability of *G. johnstoni* to brown trout.
- Breeding populations should be established by translocation to suitable streams and lakes.

### Galaxias tanycephalus

No new populations of *G. tanycephalus* were discovered during the study. The Woods Lake population was shown to be quite large, whereas the Arthurs Lake population was very small. Limnological and biological differences between the two lakes may explain this contrast. In comparison with Arthurs Lake, the more productive Woods Lake probably favours larval survival through greater food availability, and its higher turbidity may favour adult survival by reducing predation by brown trout.

The Woods Lake population also showed an unusual life history for a galaxiid. It appears to be dominated by fish one year old or less, with maturity reached after about one year. Spawning activity peaks in spring and summer, but reproductively ripe individuals occur throughout the year, suggesting an extended, possibly year-round spawning season.

### Summary of management recommendations

- Conservation status should remain as vulnerable.
- Habitat protection measures aimed at maintaining the Woods Lake population should be pursued.
- No enhancement of the brown trout population in Woods Lake should occur.
- Breeding populations should be established by translocation to suitable habitats where possible.

## Significance

The discovery and protection of new populations of *G. fontanus* and *G. johnstoni* was regarded as one of the most important outcomes of the project, because additional protected populations provide the strongest safeguard against loss from brown trout predation or local habitat disturbance.

Brown trout are permanently established in Tasmania and support an important recreational fishery, so the report frames the conservation problem as one of balancing angling interests with protection of highly restricted endemic galaxiids. The study also showed that the life histories of all three focal species were still incompletely known, meaning that continued monitoring and further breeding-biology work remained essential.
