Abstract. The Pedder galaxias (Galaxias pedderensis) from Lake Pedder, Tasmania, Australia, is one of the world’s most threatened freshwater fish. The flooding of Lake Pedder in 1972 for hydroelectric power generation caused a major change to the ecosystem that initiated an irreversible decline in the Pedder galaxias within its natural range. The flooding inundated another headwater catchment and native and introduced fish from this catchment colonised the impoundment. Numbers of the Pedder galaxias declined markedly as the impoundment matured and as colonising fish proliferated. Surveys in the 1980s confirmed the parlous state of the population, highlighting the need for conservation intervention. Severalurgent conservationactionswere undertakentosavethespecies fromextinction. Translocationwasconsidered the most important recovery action, given the critically low numbers in the wild. The species is now extinct from its natural range and is known from only two translocated populations. The conservation program, and specifically the translocation recovery action, saved the Pedder galaxias from extinction. The conservation management was extremely challenging since rapidly declining fish numbers needed timely and critical decisions to underpin the future of the fish. Recommendations are provided arising from this case study to guide conservation of freshwater fish in similar circumstances.

## Introduction

Fishes are among the most threatened groups of vertebrates (Bruton 1995). The rate of extinctions of fish has risen rapidly over the past 50 years due to a range of anthropogenic factors, including habitat loss, over-exploitation, the construction of dams and weirs, the spread of invasive species and pollution (Helfman 2007).Globally, morethan 3000fishspeciesarelisted as threatened (IUCN 2012a) and it is conservatively estimated that more than 200 species have become extinct in the past 50 years (Helfman 2007). McDowall (2006) highlights the disproportionately high percentage of galaxioid fishes listed under the IUCN criteria as threatened. In Tasmania, Australia, the

freshwater fish fauna consists of 25 species of native fish and 8 introduced species (Fulton 1990). Of the 25 native freshwater fish, the Galaxiidae is represented by 15 species, of which 11 are listed as threatened under State and/or Federal legislation (Threatened Species Section 2006).

The Pedder galaxias was the first freshwater fish listed as threatened in Tasmania. It was initially listed as Endangered by the Australian Society for Fish Biology in 1990. In 1995, the species was listed as Endangered under the Tasmanian Threatened Species Protection Act 1995 and as Extinct in the Wild in 2009 under the Commonwealth’s Environmental Protection and Biodiversity Conservation Act 1999. This paper describes the conservation recovery management of the Pedder galaxias over the past two and half decades and the actions implemented to improve the status and future of the fish. As active participants in the recovery process, we describe the challenges and issues faced, and lessons learnt, whilst attempting to prevent the imminent extinction of one of Australia’s unique native fish species.

The Pedder galaxias

The Pedder galaxias is a small scaleless freshwater fish (75–120mm fork length (FL)) (Fig. 1) described in 1968 from collections of fish made in the period 1965–67 (Frankenberg 1968; Fulton 1990). The species’ natural distribution was the headwaters of the Serpentine River, including Lake Pedder, Lake Maria and associated inflowing waters in the immediate area. The Pedder galaxias shared this restricted range with another endemic galaxiid, the swamp galaxias (Galaxias parvus). No other fish were recorded from Lake Pedder and its immediate environs (Frankenberg 1968; Andrews 1976; Sanger 1988; Hamr 1992).

Loss of the original Lake Pedder

The damming of the Serpentine and Huon rivers in 1972 initiated significant changes to the physical environment and ecology of the system that ultimately led to the demise of the Pedder galaxias. Unfortunately, the Lake Pedder environment was not scientifically documented in detail before flooding, and this limits the knowledge about specific changes in the aspects of the environment, particularly from a geomorphological perspective (Kiernan 2001). Critically, the impoundment inundated the headwaters of the Huon River catchment that contained the native climbing galaxias (Galaxias brevipinnis) and the introduced brown trout (Salmo trutta), thus initiating interactions between these species and the Pedder and swamp galaxias.

Inundationalsoledtoamajorchangeintheavailablephysical habitat and consequently the ecology of the impoundment was altered dramatically (Lake 2001). The release of nutrients from drowned vegetation and peat soils was an important driver for increases in abundances of several elements of the aquatic invertebrate community and the occurrence of filamentous algal blooms (Lake 2001). The development of the impoundment heralded changes in the fish community that was likely responding to changes in trophic structure and habitat. Recent studies on Tasmanian lacustrine galaxiids have demonstrated the importance of littoral regions of lakes to provide critical habitats for galaxiids, particularly for spawning (Hardie et al. 2006, 2007, 2011). The inundation of Lake Pedder caused immense changes to the character of the littoral zone (Lake 2001).

Decline of the Pedder galaxias

In the late 1970s, as the changes to the impoundment took effect, the population of the Pedder galaxias initially increased and large schools of juvenile fish were commonly observed around the impoundment margins (Lake 2001). Adults were also observed in streams draining into the impoundment (Crook and Sanger 1997; Lake 2001). In the late 1970s and early 1980s brown trout were taking advantage of the new impoundment and the increased abundance of prey items, including native fish, odonate larvae and other aquatic fauna (French 2011). The new impoundment, hereafter referred to as Lake Pedder, became internationally renowned as a recreational fishery, producing high catch rates of trophy-sized brown trout in the 3–8-kg range (French 2011). The high growth rate of trout appeared to have been a direct result of consumption of native fishes (French

- 2011) and the increased overall productivity within the lake associated with inundation of terrestrial soils, as observed in other parts of the world (Crisp et al. 1990). However, as the abundance of trout increased, it appears that the availability of prey (including native fish) became limiting, and consequently thecapacityofthelaketoproducetrophy-sizedtroutdiminished. This phase of high growth rate in trout lasted less than 10 years and much lower growth rates were apparent by the early 1980s.

The onlyscientific data onthe changes to thefish assemblage in the early phase of the development of the new impoundment came from Lake (1998). He conducted a long-term study of the littoral aquatic biota over the period 1975–96 that was the first such study following construction of a large impoundment in Australia (Lake 2001). The study showed a boom in the abundance of many taxa including invertebrates and the Pedder and swamp galaxias. Surveys using sweep nets indicated a rise in abundances for both endemic Galaxias species, peaking in 1977, following which no more swamp galaxias were collected, and the abundance of the Pedder galaxias rapidly declined. After 1980, Pedder galaxias disappeared entirely from the samples

and were not recorded again until more intensive, targeted surveys were undertaken using electrofishing (Fulton 1987). The climbing galaxias was not collected during Lake’s sweep net surveys, although small numbers of climbing galaxias were collected from Lake Pedder in 1983 (Sanger 1988). This probably represents the first field collection and confirmation of that species in the Lake Pedder catchment.

Contributing factors

Predation by trout, competition and aggressive behaviours by trout and the climbing galaxias have been postulated as the major factors contributing to the loss of Pedder galaxias from its habitat in Lake Pedder (Hamr 1992). Opportunistic sampling of the stomach contents of the climbing galaxias from specimens taken during field surveys provided no evidence to indicate that climbing galaxias preyed on the Pedder galaxias (Hamr 1992). The diet was dominated by terrestrial insects and aquatic insect larvae with minor contributions of aquatic crustaceans and fish. The fish were identified as juvenile swamp galaxias. The rarity of the Pedder galaxias in the wild was considered a contributing reason for notdetecting it in the diet of theclimbing galaxias and Hamr (1992) maintained that predation by the climbing galaxias was a likely causal factor in the decline of the Pedder galaxias. Similarly, McDowall and Allibone (1994) contended that predation by climbing galaxias upon common river galaxias (Galaxias vulgaris) led to the loss of the common river galaxias in streams above Lake Mahinerangi in New Zealand.

The initial colonisation by brown trout of Lake Pedder was bolstered by a single stocking of 350000 hatchery-raised brown trout fry in 1972 to supplement the recreational fishery (Inland FisheriesCommission1973;Hamr1992).Thetroutstockingwas unlikely to contribute significantly to the final outcome of trout becoming permanently established in the impoundment because the numerous inflowing streams of the new lake provided highly suitable spawning habitat for trout. Trout spawning runs were characterised by large numbers of trout with large females carrying large numbers of high-quality ova. Trout recruitment was therefore very high and the population increased rapidly.

With the expansion of trout numbers, the colonisation by the climbinggalaxiasandthedeclineofthePeddergalaxias,amajor transformation in the fish assemblage occurred. Although populations of several Tasmanian galaxiid species have coexisted for many decades with brown trout (e.g. golden galaxias (Galaxias auratus), saddled galaxias (Galaxias tanycephalus), spotted galaxias (Galaxias truttaceus)), other galaxiids cannot coexist with brown trout (e.g. Swan galaxias (Galaxias fontanus), Clarence galaxias (Galaxias johnstoni)) (Crook and Sanger 1998a, 1998b). Brown trout have also been shown to negatively impact upon the abundance of Galaxias species in situations where they coexist (e.g. spotted galaxias: Ault and White 1994). Anecdotal accounts suggest that trout preyed heavily upon the Pedder galaxias in the late 1970s; indeed, Pedder galaxias were commonly used as live bait (Terry 1994). Given this anecdotal information, and the large amount of evidence regarding the impacts of trout predation on Galaxias species more generally (McDowall 2006; Hardie et al. 2006), it appears highly likely that trout had a significant detrimental impact on the Pedder galaxias via predation.

Conservation recovery actions

Although initial recovery actions for the Pedder galaxias were undertaken in the absence of a formal recovery plan, a recovery team consisting of a range of experts in threatened species management, fisheries biology, fisheries management and key stakeholderswasformedtoleadearlyrecoveryefforts.Theteam played an important role in formulating and implementing early key recovery actions.

Field surveys

Once the parlous condition of the Pedder galaxias had been recognised, field surveys became critical for determining the status of the remaining populations.

The first comprehensive electrofishing surveys were conducted in 1988 and 1989 (Sanger 1988; Sanger and Fulton 1989). These confirmed the absence of the Pedder galaxias from the lake and established that small remnant populations remained only in four small inflowing streams – Pebbly Creek, Swampy Creek, and two creeks flowing into Bonnet Bay. Despite extensive effort, these surveys recorded a total of only 11 Pedder galaxias. In contrast, the swamp galaxias and climbing galaxias were relatively common. These results were in extreme contrast to previous observations madein the late 1970s and early 1980s, when the Pedder galaxias was highly abundant throughout the system (Lake 1998).

The survey findings prompted the Commonwealth Government to fund investigations into the decline of the Pedder galaxias, to commence recovery actions and to develop a recovery plan (Gaffney et al. 1992). Significant additional field survey effort began, with 117 sites visited in the Lake Pedder and Lake Gordon drainages during 1990–91. These surveys provided further evidence of the rapid decline of the Pedder galaxias and confirmed the locations of the few remaining populations (Hamr 1992). Fewer than 80 Pedder galaxias were collected during the 1990–91 surveys, of which 68 were collected from two streams in Bonnet Bay (Hamr 1992). During further extensive field surveys in 1992–96, only 10 individuals were collected. The last Pedder galaxias from Lake Pedder was collected on 6 March 1996 (Threatened Species Section 2006). Annual surveys were conducted after this period until 2002 (Threatened Species Section 2006).

Recovery plans

In 1992, a recovery plan for the Pedder galaxias (Gaffney et al. 1992) established conservation objectives and criteria for the species. The primary objective was to downlist the conservation status from Endangered to Vulnerable by providing the indefinite protection and enhancement of newly established populations.To meet this objective three criteriawere established: (1)a total of 5000 fish in two (new) translocated populations (Lake Oberon and one other), (2) populations in Bonnet Bay streams (LakePedderimpoundment)havereachedatleast1000fish,and (3)asignificantpopulationoccursandissustainedfor3–5years. One or more of these criteria had to be satisfied to meet the objective of the recovery plan. This initial five-year plan was followed by two further recovery plans in 1997 (Crook and Sanger 1997) and 2006 (Threatened Species Section 2006). These plans not only incorporated the recovery actions for the Pedder galaxias but were also expanded to cover all other

threatened galaxias in Tasmania. The recovery objectives and criteria for the Pedder galaxias were subject to amendments in each successive plan as the recovery program adapted to changing circumstances in the field and funding. This ensured that each plan remained adaptable to meet the challenges during its term and set achievable objectives whilst maintaining broader conservation goals.

Captive breeding and artificial fertilisation

The initial recovery plan (Gaffney et al. 1992) identified captive breeding as an important recovery action (Hamr 1992). During 1990–91, 12 fish (seven males and five females) in spawning condition were collected from streams in Bonnet Bay and released into a tank at the Salmon Ponds hatchery, New Norfolk, Tasmania. A small number of eggs were deposited in the tank but none appeared to be fertile and all failed to develop (Hamr 1992). Five adult fish also died in captivity. It was assumed that high water temperatures and stresses associated with spawning caused the deaths of these fish and the resulting poor conditions forfertilisationandeggdevelopment(Gaffneyetal.1992;Hamr 1992). The remaining seven surviving adult fish were later translocated to Lake Oberon. Two attempts at artificial fertilisation were also conducted in Spring 1991 using two females and four males collected from the Bonnet Bay streams. In total, ,1000 eggs were stripped and mixed with milt, but only 12 eggs were successfully fertilised. Eleven of these developed into viable larvae and survived to the early juvenile phase – these were subsequently translocated into Lake Oberon (Hamr 1992). The captive breeding program and artificial breeding trials did not contribute significant benefit to the recovery program. Limited resources, small number of fish and the need to prioritise higher-priority actions such as field surveys prevented the realisation of the full value of the action.

Translocation

Given the rapid decline of the Pedder galaxias within its former range, the inability to control the threatening processes, and the difficulties associated with the captive breeding trials, translocation of the few remaining fish to establish populations in new habitats was considered by the recovery team as the only viable action remaining to save the species from extinction. The early recovery planning process examined options for a translocation strategy, of which the first action was to assess the suitability of potential sites. Site evaluation was undertaken by scoring suitability against eight criteria: (1) absence of other fish, (2) presence of an inflowing stream(s), (3) abundant and diverse invertebrate assemblage, (4) diverse microhabitats, (5) water chemistry, (6) lake bathymetry and geomorphology, (7) accessibility, and (8) size of local catchment (Hamr 1992). These criteria were assessed against their similarity to Lake Pedder. Initially, efforts focussed on the creation of a single translocated population.

Strathgordon water supply dam

To lessen the risk of extinction to the species, it was considered important to establish more than one translocated population. The Strathgordon water supply dam is a small artificial impoundment (Fig. 4) built to service the needs of a local township. In 1997, preparatory work commenced on the dam to improve its suitability to sustain a translocated population of Pedder galaxias. Modifications to the site included screening of the vertical outflow pipe with fine mesh to prevent loss of transferred fish and the incursion of the climbing galaxias, and improvements to the fencing to exclude human access.

Translocation of fish from Lake Oberon into the Strathgordon water supply dam began in 2001 after it was established that the population in Lake Oberon was secure and could withstand the removal of fish. In total, 353 fish were transferred from Lake Oberon to the Strathgordon water supply dam in five translocations between 2001 and 2007 (specifically 52 fish in 2001, 22 fish in 2002, 117 fish in 2005, 53 fish in 2006 and 109 fish in 2007). To limit handling stress, biological data such as sex and fish length were not collected. Observational estimates from

dive surveys in Lake Oberon during this period indicate that fish lengths were 25–120mm FL.

During 2001–05, surveys were undertaken using a variety of methods such as snorkelling, fine-meshed fyke nets and electrofishing to detect juvenile fish spawned in the dam. Initially, the surveys were conducted every sixmonths and then reducedto an annual frequency.Translocated fishwere observedand captured (and subsequently released) during these surveys. The surveys did not provide evidence of fish recruitment and there was concern that habitat within the dam lacked the structural complexity required for successful spawning. Consequently, in 2005, installation of rock and woody debris was undertaken in the inflowing stream to create spawning habitat. Despite this, no recruitment was detected in the following breeding season. Since 2005 fish surveys using the same methods have continued to be conducted on an annual basis.

Further work to the Strathgordon water supply dam was undertaken in early 2007, with large and medium-sized rocks placed in a small section of the dam’s shoreline in an area that receivedflowing water from asmall inflow diversion.Rocks were placed to provide interstitial spaces allowing fish to easily access areas underneath rocks where eggs could be deposited. RecruitmentofPeddergalaxiaswasdetectedthefollowingyearwitheight juvenile Pedder galaxias, 40–60mm FL, collected in fine-meshed (4mm stretched mesh) fyke nets during monitoring in April 2008 (Inland Fisheries Service, unpubl. data). The timing of the recruitment suggests that the rocks placed in the littoral zone provided suitable spawning habitat. Since 2008, the population in the dam has continued to increase and several cohorts of fish have been evident during monitoring. A survey in June 2012 using 18 finemesh fyke nets, resulted in the collection of 265 Pedder galaxias overtwonights’netting,witharangeoflengthclassesrepresented: 40–140mm FL, including 27 juveniles 40–60mm FL.

While the Strathgordon water supply dam population is now well established, two climbing galaxias (166mm and 182mm FL) were detected in the dam during monitoring in 2010 and one (160mm FL) in 2011. The presence of the climbing galaxias is of concern given the possible implication in displacing the Pedder galaxias from Lake Pedder by competition and predation. Follow-up surveys in the dam and at Lake Oberon during 2012 found no additional climbing galaxias: surveillance monitoring for this species is planned to continue as an ongoing risk mitigation measure. The frequency of these surveys is yet to be determined and is contingent on the availability of funding. The survey methods will utilise fine-meshed (4mm stretched mesh) fyke nets and electrofishing at the Strathgordon water supply dam and fine-meshed (4mm stretched mesh) fyke netting and electrofishing at Lake Oberon.

Impacts of the translocations

The impact of the translocations of Pedder galaxias on the aquatic fauna of Lake Oberon and, to a lesser extent, the Strathgordon water supply dam, was recognised as a risk. A program commenced in 1991–92 to examine faunal and floral changes in Lake Oberon. Qualitative samples of aquatic fauna were collected from Lake Oberon and a control site at nearby Lake Cygnus to assess the impacts of the translocations. Initially, sampling was conducted annually; however, the frequency of sampling decreased as funding diminished or ceased. The

samples remain to be analysed and any findings have yet to be published. Impacts on aquatic biota were not detected in Lake Oberon until fish numbers had increased in the late 1990s. Ad hoc visual observations of biota during recent surveys have confirmed the abundance of the mountain shrimp (Anaspidies tasmaniae) and tadpoles have diminished considerably; however, native crayfish (Parastacoides) appear to remainrelatively unaffected.Presently,themountain shrimpis rare in thelakeand inflowingstream,withonlyoneindividualobservedina shallow section of theinflowingstream in themonitoringsurveyin2012. Previously, the mountain shrimp was abundant throughout the lake and inflowing stream. The Strathgordon water supply dam is a small, man-made impoundment with an established aquatic fauna dominated by corixiids, trichopteran larva and tadpoles. Theimpactsonaquaticfaunain thisdamhavenotbeenassessed.

Lake level management and the risk of redfin perch

Lake Pedder is a water collecting and transfer basin for the Gordon-Pedder Power Scheme, with ,40% of water in the scheme derived from the Lake Pedder catchment (Hydro Tasmania 2012). Water from Lake Pedder drains through McPartlan Canal into Lake Gordon (Fig. 2) where it is used in the Gordon Power Station at the base of the Gordon Dam. Lake Gordon has an abundant, self-sustaining population of the introduced piscivore redfin perch (Perca fluviatilis), that have been implicated in the decline of several other Tasmanian galaxiids (Crook and Sanger 1998b; Hardie et al. 2006). Currently absent from Lake Pedder, this species could inadvertently be introduced if the level of Lake Gordon rises sufficiently to submergethecanal joiningthelakes.Thisriskwasrecognised in 1996, a time when a remnant population of Pedder galaxias still existed in Lake Pedder. In recognition of this additional threat, management rules governing lake levels and water regulation were implemented to prevent incursion of redfin perch. (Inland Fisheries Commission 1996, unpublished internal report).

During 1996–98, however, water levels in Lake Gordon rose sufficiently to allow the colonisation of the canal by redfin perch. Fortunately, a small head difference between the lake levels at that time, resulted in a velocity barrier at the radial control gates at the Lake Pedder outlet and prevented movement of redfin perch into the lake. Once levels fell, a waterfall further downstream became an effective barrier, preventing any further redfin perch entering the canal. The remaining redfin perch population within the canal was eradicated in 2006 with the piscicide Rotenone. Since then, water levels have not risen to this point and a three-tiered lake level risk management strategy is being implemented by Hydro Tasmania to address future risks. While the Pedder galaxias is now believed to be extinct in Lake Pedder, it is critical that redfin perch are prevented from enteringthelakeandfurtherimpactingtheecologyofthesystem and placing the swamp galaxias at risk.

Present conservation management

Presently, conservation management is focussed on monitoring the fish populations at Lake Oberon and the Strathgordon water supply dam. Lake Oberon is irregularly monitored, due partlytothecosts ofusinghelicopterstogainaccessandbecause the population is well established and stable, and the risk of invasion by other fish species is extremely low. The methods

of monitoring fish over the past three years are presently being maintained. These involve setting 10 fine-mesh (4mm stretched mesh) fyke nets for one night and electrofishing the inflow stream and adjacent embayment. Monitoring at the Strathgordon water supply dam is conducted annually during autumn, when all fish size classes can be sampled; 20 fine-mesh (4mm stretched mesh) fyke nets are set at specific locations in the dam over two nights. The surveys provide a measure of relative abundance and surveillance of threats, such as the presence of climbing galaxias.

The reintroduction of the Pedder galaxias back into its natural range remains a highly desirable outcome in the long term but the prospects of success are considered low. The threatening factors that caused the decline of the species remain uncontrolled within the lake and mitigation of these factors would be required. A detailed assessment of the options to control the threatening factors, associated risks and costs would be required to support decision making about the reintroduction. The reintroduction of the Pedder galaxias into Lake Peddder is notbeingconsideredpresently.Protectionofthespeciesthrough education and advocacy, in addition to ongoing monitoring of the translocated populations, are likely to be the primary conservation actions into the foreseeable future.

## Discussion

The case study of the Pedder galaxias highlights several experiences and findings that should be carefully considered when managing fauna on the brink of extinction. First and foremost among these is the urgency with which management actionswere required andtheconsequentlack of opportunitiesto thoroughly research management options before acting. The need for early intervention in recovering critically endangered species on the brink of extinction is highlighted in Martin et al. (2012). In the case of the Pedder galaxias several of the recommendations, as subsequently suggested, were included in recovering the species, such as an expert and empowered recoveryteam(Milleretal.1995:Ewenetal.2013),adescriptive action-based recovery plan and decisive decisions (Martin et al.

- 2012) made in an extremely tight but timely manner. The consequences for the Pedder galaxias were not predicted

before the flooding of the original Lake Pedder and the warnings of its decline only came from general aquatic fauna surveys initiated by Lake (1998, 2001) and the Inland Fisheries Commission. It was these surveys and anecdotal observations of declining galaxias numbers from recreational trout anglers (Inland Fisheries Commission 1984), rather than systematic monitoring of the species status, that initiated a conservation program. These warning signs were acted on just in time to prevent the extinction of the Pedder galaxias.

The implementation of an ongoing program of surveillance monitoring of Lake Pedder would have provided better data and sufficient time to conduct research before implementing conservation actions. The Pedder galaxias captive breeding program, for example, was greatly hampered by the lack of knowledge of its breeding biology and our inability to fill this research gap due totherarity ofthespecies. Ongoingmonitoring of population status and demography is critically important for conservation planning and should always be a major component of threatened species management (Possingham et al. 1993; Campbell et al. 2002; Jackson et al. 2004; George et al. 2009).

The limited state of knowledge of the ecology of the Pedder galaxias presented numerous other challenges when trying to decide upon appropriate management actions (e.g. suitability of translocation sites). Ironically, the best time to undertake such research is when the plight of fish is not critical (i.e. when species are not recognised as ‘threatened’). Unfortunately, this is also the time when the need for research to obtain basic biological information is difficult to justify to conservation management agencies. Despite coming perilously close to losing the Pedder galaxias, there remains a lack of information for most Tasmanian galaxiids regarding reproductive biology, habitat requirements, interactions with other fish and susceptibility to disease (Hardie et al. 2006). This presents ongoing challenges and limitations to the management of galaxiids and their environment. Thankfully, recent studies are improving the state of knowledge, particularly in regard to water level management (Hardie et al. 2006, 2007, 2011; DPIPWE 2010).

As the options for reintroduction of the Pedder galaxias via captive breeding diminished in the early stages of the recovery process, translocation emerged as the only viable option for preventing the species’ extinction. The use of translocation as a conservation management tool for threatened fishes has been used widely over many years, e.g. the Swan galaxias (Galaxias fontanus) in Tasmania, Australia (Crook and Sanger 1997), and the barred galaxias in Victoria, Australia (Ayres et al. 2012a), but it remains controversial due to the potential effects on the receiving ecosystems (Arthington 1991; Maitland 1995; Minckley 1995; Gillespie and Hero 1999). Growing concern relating to these matters has prompted the development of a range of translocation policies and guidelines to identify issues and best practices to increase the likelihood of success and reduce the risks and impacts associated with translocation (Williams et al. 1988; Commonwealth of Australia 1999; George et al. 2009; Ayres et al. 2012b; IUCN 2012b).

The potential risks of translocating the Pedder galaxias were recognised and carefully considered by the recovery team in the inital recovery process. Foremost among these was the potential effects to the ecology of the receiving ecosystem at Lake Oberon. Recognising that introduction of fish to a fish-free, pristine lake would have strong ecological effects, the decision to use this location was based upon an appraisal of the conservation significance of the lake’s fauna relative to the benefits of saving the Pedder galaxias. Moreover, the rarity of the Pedder galaxias in the wild meant that urgent action was required and many translocation issues could not be addressed to the extent that would be required in contemporary fish translocation guidelines and policies in Australia. The perilous circumstances of the species meant that judgements and decisions were made with imperfect, incomplete, or without relevant, information. Obviously, this type of decision can be strongly contested and was controversial, but in this case the critical status of the Pedder galaxias carried significant weight in the decision process. A short-coming of the Pedder galaxias recovery process to date has been the lack of a detailed, quantitative and ongoing assessment of the effects of the translocations upon the fauna of the receiving ecosystems. Such an assessment would be valuable for informing future translocations of threatened fishes and to understand the broader ecological consequences of fish translocation as a conservation action. This work was not completed due to competing priorities with other

actions that were considered of more direct importance to the fish.

Another important issue to consider in the recovery of species facing imminent extinction are the genetic consequences of a very small population size. Small population size can result in low genetic diversity, inbreeding depression and accumulation of mutations, thus lowering evolutionary potential, compromising reproductive fitness and reducing long-term populationviability(Lande1988;Spielmanetal.2004;Frankham 2005). The implementation of conservation actions for the Pedder galaxias commenced at a time when fish numbers had already declined to critical levels and the populations at Lake Oberon andtheStrathgordonwatersupplydam wereestablished from just 34 individuals translocated from the two Bonnet Bay streams. This founder population is well below the minimum population size required to avoid adverse genetic effects on population viability in other taxa(Frankham 2005). However, as these were the only individuals that could be collected despite extensive survey effort, the recovery team had no choice but to proceed with the translocation using the available fish. Previous research on the genetics of Tasmanian lacustrine galaxiids has demonstrated a lack of mtDNA diversity, suggesting that very severe, but transitory, population bottlenecks due to glacial landlocking may have been a relatively common occurrence in the past (Ovenden and White 1990). Such findings provide some support that the translocated Pedder galaxias populations will remain viable in the longer term; however, a specific investigation into the population genetics of the Pedder galaxias and the impact of the population size has not been conducted. To date, there is no evidence (e.g. mutations) to suggest any adverse genetic effects. However, a detailed genetic analysis of the translocated populations wouldbe of great benefit forincreasing understanding of the genetic implications of such a small founder population for re-establishing a threatened species. It would appear that the long-term evolutionary pathway for the species has been altered inexorably and that the adaptive processes operating in the translocation locations will determine the evolutionary fate of the species into the future.

One fundamental aspect of the management of the Pedder galaxias was the capacity for conservation management actions to remain flexible and adaptive to changing circumstances. Initial conservation management was guided by a recovery team representing a range of agencies and stakeholders. Regular meetings by this group proved invaluable in the early phases of the program for addressing critical issues as the recovery process developed, particularly withregards to the translocation. Later, the recovery team played an important role in establishing the framework of goals, objectives and targets in the recovery plan and overseeing its implementation. Adaptive management principles and an acceptance of the need to act in the absence of important information were instrumental in saving the Pedder galaxias. Although now extinct in its natural range and habitat, the fish survives in two distinctly different and geographically separated habitats. These populations require an ongoing program of monitoring to provide information on population trends and surveillance against new threats. Without such a program, the issues of inadequate time and information may well reemerge if the species’ status declines once more in the future.

There has been much debate over many years about the potential of restoring Lake Pedder to its original form by

draining the impoundment (Sharples 2001). The idea remains highly controversial and is clearly complex, with many significant challenges (Lake 2001). With respect to the Pedder galaxias, the restoration of the lake is unlikely to recreate conditions favourable for its successful reintroduction. The elimination of trout and particularly the climbing galaxias would be extremely difficult, and if these could not be eradicated then Lake Pedder may remain a hostile environment for the Pedder galaxias (Sanger 2001). Moreover, there has been a poor success rate of reintroductions of species worldwide and more strategic approaches would need to be adopted to improve success (Armstrong and Seddon 2008). Armstrong and Seddon (2008) provide a structured framework to guide reintroductions based on consideration of factors such as habitat effects on population establishment and persistence. The reintroduction of the Pedder galaxias into the lake would need to be carefully considered and planned, with significant attention to the assessment of the likelihood of success balanced against the conservation benefits and resourcing costs associated with future management, before decisionscould be made (Haightet al. 2000). Significant funding would be required to control alien fish and make Lake Pedder suitable for reintroducing the Pedder galaxias. Also, the conservation security of the Pedder galaxias and the cost–benefits of implementingactionssuchasthereintroductionintoLakePedder would need to be balanced against the needs of other threatened freshwater fish (predominantly galaxiids) in Tasmania.

Regarding future management, a ‘Project Prioritisation Protocol’ (Joseph et al. 2009) has been used to rank conservation actions for threatened species in Tasmania (DPIPWE 2010). This approach aspires to provide a consistent and transparent approach for prioritising recovery actions across a range of threatened species based on project benefit, likelihood of success and cost. In the context of threatened species conservation within Tasmania, the objective is to secure, in the wild, the greatestnumberofthreatenedTasmaniantaxaaspossiblewithin a fifty-year period based on delivering the most cost-efficient outcomes (DPIPWE 2010). As a result of this process, the Pedder galaxias has been acknowledged as a priority species (DPIPWE 2010), with the primary management actions identified as the need to continue monitoring of the Lake Oberon and Strathgordon water supply dam populations and the identification, assessment and establishment of a third translocation site. Despite this outcome, it is highly probable that the Lake Oberon population, by virtue of its geographical isolation, will remain themainstayforthesurvivalofthespecies,withtheStrathgordon water supply dam being a secondary and less secure population. However, conservation prospects could be improved by creating additional populations. Hamr (1992) provided a list of potential translocation sites and additional sites are known; although the suitability of these additional sites is yet to be assessed. Nevertheless, before any translocation, a thorough assessment of impacts and risks would need to be conducted using existing protocols and guidelines as a basis (Commonwealth of Australia 1999; Ayres et al. 2012b; IUCN 2012b). The adoption of quantitative population viability analysis approaches to assessing risk and alternative management options would also be highly beneficial.

Originally the subject of a single recovery plan, the Pedder galaxias recovery was managed under a multispecies plan from 1997, targeting several threatened Tasmanian galaxiids.

Multispecies recovery planning and management arose in the 1990s, mainly as a response of the US Fish and Wildlife Service to the US Endangered Species Act (Jewell 2000; Clark and Harvey 2002). There has been considerable debate about the relative merits of single versus multispecies recovery management (Franklin 1993; Jewell 2000; Boersma et al. 2001; Clark and Harvey 2002). Multispecies plans often reflect economy in recovery management resourcing rather than genuine integration of management actions. Clark and Harvey (2002), reviewing US multispecies plans from the 1990s, concluded that they:

led to fewer recovery tasks per species than single-species plans; often reflected a weaker understanding of individual species’ autecology; had a weaker focus on adaptive management; were updated less frequently.

A targeted single-species approach was used here, even within the context of later multispecies plans, since each species had distinct management needs.

Single-species management in this case prevented the first known fish species extinction in Australia. The Pedder galaxias recovery demonstrates the amount of sustained effort required to bringasinglefishspeciesbackfromthebrinkofextinctioninwhat is a relatively simple environmental, legislative and political context. Whilst recognising the need for integrated ecosystem and landscape approaches, we believe that the Pedder galaxias story provides a demonstration of the important role singlespecies recovery actions can play in targeted conservation.
