## Introduction

The original Lake Pedder in South West Tasmania was inhabited by two species of native galaxid fishes: the Pedder galaxias (Galaxias pedderensis) and the Swamp galaxias (Galaxias parvus). Both species were endemic to the lake and its feeder streams (Andrews, 1976). The lake was inundated following the building of a large hydroelectric scheme which involved the damming of the Gordon, Serpentine and Huon rivers. Following an initial population explosion of galaxiids in the new lake, subsequent observations showed that numbers were declining. The most recent investigations by the Inland Fisheries Commission (IFC) have shown that although the numbers of G. parvus remain relatively high in the region, populations of G. pedderensis have undergone a dramatic decline in the new lake as well as surrounding streams (Sanger 1988, Sanger & Fulton 1989). Electrofishing surveys of the new Lake Pedder in 1988 - 89 revealed that few G. pedderensis remained. The species was absent in the new lake and was found in small numbers in the following four creeks only:

- 1. Pebbly Creek (1988), 1 specimen; no further specimens in 1989.
- 2. Swampy Creek (1989), 1 specimen
- 3. Bonnet Bay Creek 1 (1989), 3 specimens
- 4. Bonnet Bay Creek 2 (1989), 6 specimens
This represents a drastic reduction in the original range and population numbers from those reported by investigators prior to flooding. Frankenberg (1968) had found the species to be abundant in the old lake and inflowing streams while Andrews (1976 & pers. comm.) observed large numbers of fish in Stillwater Rivulet, ,Swampy Creek, the upper Serpentine River, Trappes Rivulet and the old lake. Following the inundation of the old lake, Causby, Fulton and Lake (pers. comms.) all reported large numbers of adults and juveniles in the new lake between 1975 and 1980. The cause of this recent decline is not clear, however the introduction of brown trout, Salmo trutta, a large predator as well as a possible competitor for food, may be at least partly responsible. Additionally, and perhaps more importantly, the new lake and the adjoining streams have been invaded by another potential competitor and predator, the climbing galaxias - Galaxias brevipinnis (Sanger & Fulton 1989, This study). Galaxias brevipinnis was formerly not reported from the area immediately adjacent to the old lake but is now very abundant throughout the Pedder and Gordon drainages. As a result, G. pedderensis has become the most endangered fish species in Tasmania and could become extinct in the near future if appropriate management steps are not undertaken immediately. The species has been listed officially as endangered by the Australian Society for Fish Biology in 1989 (Harris 1990). This report documents progress achieved in the assessment of the conservation status and the management of Galaxias pedderensis under the conditions of a two year ANPWS funded project. This report serves to provide a basis for the proposed Recovery Plan for the species. The project was funded under the Endangered Species Program. Relevant documentation is in Appendix 3.

## Methods

### Population Surveys

An intensive electrofishing survey of creeks running into the new lake was undertaken from February 1990 to May 1990 to establish the number of remaining populations and the status of known populations of G. pedderensis as well as the abundance and distribution of G. brevipinnis and G. parvus. Some additional surveys were also conducted from September 1990 to May 1991. An initial intensive survey was undertaken between February 12 and May 1, 1990. The last additional site was surveyed on 12.3.91. All significant permanent water courses within the Lake Pedder - Serpentine drainage (and some within the Lake Gordon drainage) were visited at least once, some on several occasions during summer when flows were low. Sites were sampled by a team of two people working upstream then downstream with a Smith-Root Model 12 backpack electroshocker at voltages ranging from 600 to 900 V and at 60 to 120 Hz.

### Life History

The life history of Galaxias brevipinnis, G. parvus and Salmo trutta were examined in the Lake Pedder area to establish their reproductive and feeding biology as well as to examine the mechanisms by which these species may have affected the abundance of G. pedderensis. Monthly samples of G. brevipinnis and G. parvus were taken from McPartlan Creek 3. Additional samples were taken from adjacent creeks in the McPartlan Pass and Strathgordon areas when necessary (ie: when fish numbers were low in the main sampling site). Collections of G. pedderensis held by the Tasmanian Museum and the Inland Fisheries Commission were examined to compare life history, ecology (feeding biology and habitat) prior to and after the inundation of the lake. Dissected G. pedderensis were treated as outlined for the other two galaxid species. Both Bonnet Bay Creeks were visited at least once a month from August 1990 to May 1991 in order to estimate the number of fish in each population as well as to gain information about the distribution and habits of the remaining fish. During and following the breeding season intensive field work involving visual surveys, electroshocker sampling and drift netting was undertaken in an attempt to find eggs and/or larvae of G. pedderensis in the wild. Most G. pedderensis captured during the study were measured and released but some were kept for laboratory observation and laboratory and fed a diet of live aquatic insect larvae. Any G. pedderensis which died in captivity were either dissected and preserved in 70 % ethanol or preserved whole in liquid nitrogen for future electrophoretic analysis. Length frequency distributions were plotted by season and over the whole study period for various populations of each species in order to determine growth patterns. The sacullus otoliths of a number of fish (preserved in 70 % ethanol) of each Galaxias species were also examined to estimate the age at given size. Otoliths of fish of a representative size range were examined for each species. Otoliths were dissected out and cleared in glycerol. The rings and hyaline zones on the otoliths were counted and fish were aged by methods outlined in previous aging studies of other galaxiid fishes (Humphries 1989, Bonnett 1990). Ages determined from otoliths were also compared with modes in the size-frequency data.

### Taxonomy

Collections of G. pedderensis held by Monash University, the Tasmanian Museum and the Inland Fisheries Commission were examined to compare morphology prior to and after the inundation of the lake. The possibility that hybridization with G. brevipinnis could have occurred was examined. Ten G. pedderensis and 18 G. brevipinnis, selected from those specimens collected within the Pedder drainage, were X-rayed and the number of vertebrae were counted (counts excluded hypural centra as in McDowall and Frankenberg, 1981). Counts from both species in this selection, together with vertebral counts made by Dr Andrew Sanger (IFC) in 1988 (11 G. pedderensis and 22 G. brevipinnis), were compared in conjunction with counts made in previous taxonomic studies of the two species (Andrews 1976, McDowall and Frankenberg 1981).

### Captive breeding

During early August 1990, an artificial stream flowing into a large (2000 litre) fibreglass holding tank was constructed at the Commission's Salmon Ponds hatchery compound (Fig.1). The system was filled with Lake Pedder water which was recirculated using a large pump. The water was drawn through a biological filter in the bottom of the pond which consisted of layers of fine and coarse gravel over a fine mesh. Water draining through the filter was pumped back to the head of the stream where it re-entered in a turbulent, re-aerating resurge. Substrate and vegetation collected from streams flowing into Lake Pedder was used to construct a range of microhabitats within the tank and stream similar to those found in the natural environment. These were: snag pile, rock pile, sedge patch, open sand, macrophyte patch, chaotic boulder and gravel riffles. The pond and stream were shaded by a cover of 50% green shade cloth to reduce ambient light (natural habitat was always heavily shaded) and reduce fright responses to human activity. During 1990, the water in the system was kept at ambient temperature but from spring 1991 the water was cooled by recirculation through a refrigeration unit to keep temperatures between 10 and 15°C. Temperature was monitored daily. Oxygen was monitored at intervals using a WTW pre-calibrated metera. Dissolved oxygen was always high (>80% saturation). early spring 1990 and 1991) were introduced into the tank immediately after capture with a short period of acclimation to the pond temperature. Behaviour and condition of the fish were regularly monitored and observations on spawning and egg deposition were recorded, all with minimal disturbance.

## Results and Discussion

## 1. Survey of species distributions

Overall, 117 locations around the new Lake Pedder and Lake Gordon were surveyed (see Appendix 1). Table 1 summarizes the occurrences of G. pedderensis, G. parvus, S. trutta and G. brevipinnis. G. pedderensis G. pedderensis was found to be extremely rare in the Lake Pedder area. Among the newly surveyed sites only one new location for G. pedderensis was found (Stillwater Rivulet). At this new site only one individual was recorded; a large adult male. Electrofishing and trapping (on five subsequent occasions) failed to yield any more G. pedderensis, but two G. brevipinnis were captured. It appears therefore that there are few, if any, G. pedderensis left within this stream. It has also been established that while two of the streams where G. pedderensis was previously found (Bonnet Bay Creeks 1 and 2, 1988 - 89 surveys) still contain some individuals of the species, it appears to have disappeared altogether from the other two streams surveyed (Pebbly and Swampy creeks, one individual in each during 198889). No further individuals were found in Pebbly and Swampy creeks but G. pedderensis were found consistently in Bonnet Bay Creeks 1 and 2. Prior to the flooding of Lake Pedder, the species was abundant in Lake Pedder, Lake Maria and the Serpentine River and its tributary streams such as Maria Creek, Swampy Creek, Trappes Creek, Hermit Creek and Stillwater Rivulet (Frankenberg 1968, Bayly et al. 1972, Andrews 1976, McDowall & Frankenberg 1981). Following the inundation of the old lake, G. pedderensis was abundant in the new lake through the 1970's (Andrews, 1976; McDowall & Frankenberg, 1981; Fulton pers. comm; Causby pers comm). G. parvus G. parvus was found throughout the streams flowing into Lake Pedder from Strathgordon in the north, to Scotts Peak in the south. G. parvus was also found in the shallow flooded edges of Lake Pedder and in large pools on the button-grass plain adjacent to the lake. Although G. parvus was found in fewer creeks than G. brevipinnis and S. trutta, , its populations had high densities of fish of all sizes and the species can therefore be considered to be common and abundant in the Lake Pedder drainage. Its continued abundance may be due to its habitat preference, its life history (not having a migratory juvenile stage), as well as its ability to colonize the newly flooded swampy areas of the lake which are not suitable habitats for trout or the Climbing and Peddei galaxiids. Furthermore its original distribution was not confined to the Pedder Serpentine drainage and thus some of its populations may not have been affected by the flooding of this region. G. brevipinnis G. brevipinnis S was the most common species found during the survey .It was distributed throughout the streams flowing into Lake Pedder (from Strathgordon in the north to Scotts Peak in the south). G. brevipinnis densities were generally higher in fast flowing, steep gradient streams with rocky substrate. Stream populations had high densities of fish of all sizes and the species can therefore be considered to be common and abundant in the area. One large population was found in Lake Pedder itself, on an artificial rocky shore immediately adjacent to the Scotts Peak Dam. A number of G. brevipinnis were also collected around the shallow sandy shore of Ted's Beach near Strathgordon. In some of the larger slow flowing streams (such as Bonnet Bay Ck. 2 and Swampy Ck.) G. brevipinnis was absent in the lower portions but very abundant in the shallow faster flowing upper reaches. As a result of this habitat preference, the species was often found in the same streams as trout and G. parvus. G. brevipinnis was also common and abundant in streams draining into Lake Gordon vhere it appears to be the sole galaxiid species. It is likely that the species has spread from the Lake Gordon drainage following the inundation of lake Pedder. G. brevipinnis had been recorded from the upper reaches of the Gordon and Huon Rivers prior to the flooding of Lake Pedder (Andrews, 1976). S. trutta S. trutta was also common and abundant throughout Lake Pedder and Lake Gordon. It occurred mainly in the larger rivers and streams entering the two lakes where it is abundant and most often the sole occupant. S. trutta also occurs as a transient resident in some of the smaller streams with adults migrating upstream to spawn from autumn to mid-winter and fry hatching in spring and remaining in the streams during the summer known to exist in both lakes. Large adults (up to 2.5 kg) were observed and collected from streams during the autumn spawning run in streams such as Sandfly Creek, Stinky Creek, Bonnet Bay Creeks 1 &2, Twin Creeks and Trappes Creek. It is known that initial colonisation occurred from the Gordon and Huon rivers where trout were known to occur in large numbers prior to the inundation of the original Lake Pedder (Lynch 1972a & b). Additionally, in September 1972, the Inland Fisheries Commission liberated 350 000 S. trutta fry in the new Lake Pedder. This liberation of brown trout fry was expected to augment the existing stocks of brown trout and thus develop a viable recreational fishery (Lynch 1972b)

### A. Galaxias pedderensis:

### Spawning and reproduction

Field sampling and captive breeding trials from two successive breeding seasons (1990 & 1991) show that this species spawns in spring (early October) when water temperatures first begin to rise. Between 28 August and 24 September, 1990, six G. pedderensis in breeding condition (3 males and 3 females with large gonads, ie: Stage IV) were collected from Salmon Ponds. Some of these fish eventually spawned within the tank between artificial stream section but some eggs were also deposited on rocks and vegetation within the pond section itself. None of the spawned eggs appeared to be fertile and all failed to develop (see section 6) In 1991 10 adult G. pedderensis in breeding condition (5 males and 5 females with large gonads, ie: Stage IV) were collected from the two Bonnet Bay creeks between August 29 and September 27 (see Table 2). On October 14, a mating pair as well as a freshly spawned female were found in Creek 2 (see Table 2) and it is therefore assumed that spawning in the field had begun just prior to that date.The water temperature in the creeks ranged from 6.5 to 7.5 °C between the end of September and mid October when spawning is presumed to take place. Some of the above fish were transferred to the spawning tank at Salmon Ponds and eventually spawned within the tank between artificial stream section and were associated with vegetation. Eggs were deposited under flat rocks as well as underneath aquatic vegetation and submerged driftwood. Some eggs were also deposited on rocks and vegetation within the pond section itself but these appeared to be mainly minor sites with only a few eggs present at each site. As was the case in spring 1990, all eggs failed to develop. ## 5. Effect of Introduced Species on G. pedderensis.

A. Competition: Because of the expansion of G. brevipinnis and S. trutta within the Pedder catchment, the frequent overlap in habitat requirements at the scale of a stream reach and the opportunistic carnivorous nature of their diets, there is a strong likelihood of competition for food and space between these two species and G. pedderensis. The distribution survey conducted in this study has shown that both G. brevipinnis and S. trutta have replaced G. pedderensis in most of the tributaries of the new lake. G. brevipinnis is of particular concern because it has invaded even the smaller streams (which are not suitable for S. trutta) and apparently displaced G. pedderensis in these streams. It is highly probable that.G. brevipinnis, which is naturally found in fast flowing steep streams, is better suited to the upper portions of streams which remain after the flooding of the original lake and its surrounding plain. In addition to this potential habitat-induced competitive advantage, G. brevipinnis also l has a much higher fecundity than G. pedderensis (Fig. 4) and once a stream is colonized its numbers may increase dramatically within one or two breeding seasons. This problem is compounded by the fact that the remaining larger streams (such as Swampy, Hermit and Trappes Creeks) which are less suitable for G. brevipinnis, have been occupied by resident populations of S. trutta. Having lost most of its original slow-flowing lowland habitat, G. pedderensis is therefor possibly unable to compete against the introduced species in the remaining stream habitats. The diets of G. brevipinnis and S. trutta are very similar to that of G. pedderensis (see section 3) and these two invading species must therefore be regarded as significant potential competitors for food resources. Although the diet of G. parvus is also similar to that of G. pedderensis, competition is less likely to occur between the two species because of their different habitat preferences. It is possible however that some competition occurs in areas where G. parvus is abundant. Furthermore, the larvae of G. brevipinnis, G. parvus and S. trutta emerge earlier in the year than G. pedderensis and their young may therefore be better able to compete for resources during their first summer of life because of their larger size. B. Predation: Although no G. pedderensis were found in the stomachs of G. brevipinnis or S. trutta, both species are likely to be major predators of G. pedderensis. S. trutta has been shown to prey species (Sanger and Fulton 1991, Fletcher 1979). There are also numerous anecdotal reports of large trout preying on G. pedderensis during the first 10 years following the flooding of the original Lake Pedder. Trout would prey on galaxiids, particularly in the lake. Additionally, during periods of high water levels in the impoundment (as was the case in spring 1991), large trout enter the flooded lower portions of the creeks to forage and may constitute a threat to resident G. pedderensis. Since G. brevipinnis has been shown to prey on juvenile G. parvus in the field and laboratory it must also be considered a potentially serious threat to at least the smaller sizeclasses of G. pedderensis. Both G. parvus and G. brevipinnis have been shown 0 consume freshly spawned ova during their reproductive seasons and must therefore be considered as potential predators of G. pedderensis ova. Additionally, as previously mentioned, the larvae of G. brevipinnis, G. parvus and S. trutta emerge earlier in the year, and by the t time G. pedderensis hatch the larvae of these species have grown large enough to act as potential predators. 6. Management and Conservation of G. pedderensis. A. Management Options After assessing the present status of the existing populations of G. pedderensis it is I. clear that the species has undergone a dramatic decline in numbers as well as range within its habitat. The remaining populations in Bonnet Bay Creeks 1 and 2 are vulnerable and therefore in need of careful management. There are three management options to be considered: 1. Protection of existing populations; 2. Translocation of stock to secure habitats; 3. Captive breeding to increase existing and/ or translocated populations. Option 1: Protection of existing populations. The protection of existing populations of G. pedderensis must be considered as the least feasible option as it is both difficult and time consuming. The numbers of invading brevipinnis spawn within the creeks and must therefore be considered a serious threat to the survival of the resident G. pedderensis. Continued removal of these introduced fish would be very difficult however, due to the size and complexity of the habitat and the low effeicency of elctrofishing under these conditions (estimated to be less than 25% for one pass of the shocker, Davies, IFC pers. comm.). It would also be time consuming and, with the annual funding and personnel commitment annual commitment necessary, would be difficult to sustain well into the future. Additionally, continued frequent disturbance and electroshocking of the streams could affect the resident G. pedderensis. The lower sections of both Bonnet Bay creeks are of low gradient with relatively wide valley floors with large areas at low elevation above the stream banks. Larger floods can spread widely across the valley floor and have the potential to by-pass any barrier within the channel, allowing fish passage. The construction of a barrier to fish passage near the mouth of the stream is not considered a practical option because of this stream topography. Any such barrier would be unable to stop the passage of G. brevipinnis, already found in large numbers in the upper portions of both creeks due to the topographical restrictions on its maximum drop (< 3m). G. brevipinnis is known to climb vertical faces as high as 5Om on an annual basis (Fulton and Davies, IFC, pers. comm.). Furthermore, G. pedderensis is thought to be migratory to and from the lake and a barrier could therefore hamper any such migration and be detrimental to the survival of the species. All introduced fish captured in the course of regular sampling were removed from the two creeks and during autumn 1990 and 1991, efforts were made to remove as many spawning trout as possible in order to reduce the number of juvenile trout within the streams during the following summer. This strategy appears to have been at least partly successful as fewer juvenile trout were captured in the summer of 1990-91 than in The continued removal of G. brevipinnis on the other hand seems to have had no effect on the abundance of this species in the two creeks. Since there appear to be large breeding populations in the upper reaches of both streams, it is unlikely that G. brevipinnis could be eliminated or even controlled within the two catchments. G. brevipinnis occurs throughout Creek 1 but in Creek 2 it appears to be mainly restricted to the upper portions. It is therefore likely that G. brevipinnis will eventually displace G. pedderensis in Creek 1 and it can only be hoped that G. brevipinnis will remain restricted to the upper reaches of Creek 2 and thus allow G. pedderensis to survive in the lower reaches. Option 2: Translocation. Translocation of a portion of the wild population and/or captive bred fish to safe habitats free of any introduced fish or human interference is considered a primary option. Ideally, a remote lake with at least some similarities to the old Lake Pedder (ie: a sandy bottom, a wide range of microhabitats, abundant cover and inflowing streams) should be chosen as a site to which G. pedderensis could be translocated. Criteria deemed necessary for a translocation site are discussed in the Methods. The suitability of a number of lakes examined in the World Heritage Area is shown in Table 7. It was decided that Lake Oberon in the Western Arthur Range provided all the features Tequired of an ideal translocation site and was therefore considered the best choice (Table 7). It has undercut banks, a sandy bottom and abundant vegetation which provide ample sites suited for spawning and flow appears perennial. The mouth and lower reaches of this stream provide habitats suitable for adult and juvenile fish alike (rock and vegetative cover). Option 3: Captive breeding and artificial fertilisation Captivebreeding In spring 1990, six G. pedderensis in breeding condition (3 males and 3 females with large gonads, ie: Stage IV) were collected from the two Bonnet Bay creeks and introduced into the spawning tank at Salmon Ponds. These fish eventually spawned but none of the eggs spawned developed and did not appear to be fertile. It is assumed that the males failed to fertilize the egg nests. Under conditions in the wild there is a migration to sites where the adults congregate and spawn and it is possible that the artificial pond did not provide the right cues for successful spawning and/or survival of fertilized eggs. Additionally the temperature regime within the artificial system may have failed to provide the appropriate cues needed for reproduction, with some fluctutations noted between 15 and 20°C. In 1991, six fish (4 males, 2 females) were collected and introduced to the pond in autumn (April - May) to allow for greater acclimation time in captivity and another five fish (3 males, 2 females) were added in early spring (August - September). To eliminate temperature fluctuations, the water temperature in the pond was controlled using a thermostat controlled refrigeration unit and the temperature was held successfully between 10 and 15 °C. As in 1990, some of the fish (at least 3 of the females) spawned in the stream (see section 3A for details) but again the eggs failed to develop. The reason for this was unknown but problems related to acclimation and high or fluctuating temperatures had been eliminated. Males and females were also seen together in the stream portion but it is not known whether pairing occurred. In 1990, five of the captive fish died after reproducing but in 1991 all of the captive fish survived and were included amongst those translocated to Lake Oberon (see below). It is assumed that the deaths were associated with high temperatures in the pond coupled with spawning stress.

### Translocation

Two translocations of G. pedderensis have been performed into Lake Oberon and a monitoring program initiated. Translocation 1: on November 1 1991, 18 adult G. pedderensis were released into Lake Oberon. For the translocation from Salmon Ponds to Lake Oberon, the fish were transported by in the free space above the water. The bags were then placed into plastic buckets and transported with a minimum of disturbance. Once at Lake Oberon, the fish were partially acclimated by floating the bags for three hours in the lake near the mouth of the stream. Prior to release, all fish were passed through a series of three washings with Lake Oberon water with minimal transfer of water between each washing in order to reduce the risk of co-introduction of planktonic organisms. The original transportation water (from the rearing pond) was taken back to Hobart. The three washings were disposed of in scrub high on the eastern side of the valley, at a sufficient distance to eliminate any chance of organisms migrating to the lake and well away from any potential ponding depression or yabbie burrows. Of the 18 fish released, nine were mature males (FL: 70, 118, 88, 108, 116, 76, 79, 70 and 100 mm), eight were mature females (FL: 85, 78, 112, 125, 122, 120, 120 and 110 mm) and one was an unsexed immature fish (FL 85 mm). All fish appeared healthy and no signs of stress were observed as the fish adjusted to their new surroundings. As at least one of the large females still had not released her eggs and all of the males were ripe (ie: running milt), there is a possibility that spawning may still have occurred at Lake Oberon after release.

### Translocation 2

On 5/2/1992, a further 12 fish were released into Lake Oberon. 11 fish were juveniles from the artificial breeding program reared in aquaria in Hobart. One was a wild-collected adult from Bonnet Bay Creek 1. The release protocol was as for Translocation 1.
