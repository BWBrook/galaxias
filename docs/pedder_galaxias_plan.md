# Pedder galaxias research plan

## Background (From collaborator Chris Burridge)

I've been thinking about Galaxiid fishes a bit more lately, including Pedder galaxias. Maybe in the same vein as mainland declines of thylacines, devils.... etc.

### The Lake Pedder story in <100 words

*Galaxias pedderensis* endemic to Lake Pedder. Lake flooded for hydro. At that time brown trout and climbing galaxias (*Galaxias brevipinnis*) enter lake. Story goes that pedderensis populations boomed immediately after flooding (more food; common lake flooding phenomenon), but at the same time were predated by trout and perhaps large brevipinnis, plus competition for food from both species. There was also some trout stocking for the recreational fishery. Then pedderensis started declining, and brevipinnis increasing. Eventually, pedderensis goes extinct in the lake (translocated to two other sites, where it survives).

The anti-trout crew would like to think it was brevipinnis that caused the extinction of pedderensis. The trout fishermen would like to think it was the brevipinnis that did the damage.

So, maybe a modelling approach could indicate which it was, or if both were required? Should be a lot of trout information (growth, fecundity, densities) to work with, and perhaps some for brevipinnis..... data from other galaxiids could be used as a proxy for pedderensis.

Perhaps not much hope for pedderensis in Lake Pedder into the future (re-introduction of pedderensis, management of brevs and/or trout), but perhaps still an informative activity for other systems. Some novelty in this species as well (was arguably Australia's most threatened fish), to raise profile of work...... almost 'the thylacine of fishes'.

Was thinking there also might be outputs along the lines of trout and brev densities at which pedderensis goes under.

Another interesting story is Galaxias johnstoni in Clarence Lagoon. They stock it with Brook trout so fisherman can catch something there, and don't introduce brown or rainbow trout themselves. However, Brook trout do eat the galaxiids, but not as lethal as brown or rainbow trout. 

The Clarence Lagoon study is perhaps more a "was it a fluke they got the stocking density right to enable coexistence"? So, there is a bit of a balancing act in terms of fisherman satisfaction and galaxiid survival..... how many Brook trout do they stock it with for best outcomes (Brook trout don't breed in there, reputedly). It's also involves a fish with your name on it.....

## Thoughts on modelling (from me: Barry Brook)

My first reaction is that the Pedder idea is the stronger scientific story, and yes, I think a simulation approach is the right way to go. I would frame it not as trying to identify a single culprit with courtroom certainty, but as a retrospective causal-attribution problem: which mechanism set can reproduce the observed chronology under biologically plausible parameters? That chronology is already suggestive: Pedder galaxias appears to have boomed immediately after inundation, trout predation was noted early, climbing galaxias became the dominant galaxiid later, and Pedder disappeared from its natural range. Sanger explicitly floated the idea that Pedder may already have been declining before climbing galaxias became abundant, so climbing galaxias may have hastened rather than initiated the collapse; current official summaries likewise treat the cause as unclear but most likely involving predation and competition from both brown trout and climbing galaxias, rather than a clean either/or.  ([Tasmanian DNR&E][1])

So I would not build a neat closed-form model and hope for a decisive analytical answer. I’d build a stochastic, mechanistic simulator that is mathematical underneath but designed for calibration against sparse historical evidence. A simple Lotka–Volterra exercise would probably miss the key biology. The minimum sensible structure, to my mind, is a stage-structured Pedder galaxias population split between main-lake habitat and refuge tributaries, plus brown trout, plus climbing galaxias, plus a simple habitat/productivity state representing the short-lived post-flood enrichment pulse. The split between lake and tributaries matters because the species was eventually restricted to a few streams rather than simply vanishing everywhere at once. Stage structure matters because Pedder galaxias is a low-fecundity, non-diadromous fish that matures at roughly two years, breeds mostly at three to four years, and lives up to about six years.  ([Tasmanian DNR&E][1])

In practice I’d let four processes drive the dynamics. First, a flood pulse temporarily raises Pedder recruitment or survival after inundation. Second, trout pressure rises through colonisation plus stocking; the later review paper notes release of 350,000 brown trout fry in late 1972, and the current listing says trout numbers increased rapidly during the first decade after flooding. Third, climbing galaxias colonises and increases with a lag, eventually becoming dominant in many inflowing streams. Fourth, Pedder recruitment and survival are hit by some combination of trout predation, climbing-galaxias competition, and possibly direct predation by large climbing galaxias. I would also include a habitat-only null model, but mainly as a foil, because Sanger argued that collapse of food supply or water quality alone was unlikely to explain the decline.   ([Tasmanian DNR&E][1])

The part I’d do before any serious coding is to build an evidence ledger: every dated observation turned into a machine-readable constraint with a source, uncertainty score, taxonomic confidence score, and spatial tag. One especially worthwhile front-end task would be re-screening museum lots, because Sanger noted that some early abundance statements may have conflated Pedder and climbing galaxias until vertebral counts were used to separate them. That ledger would include things like pre-flood abundance, the early boom, anglers noting trout eating galaxiids in the 1970s, the museum-based shift from Pedder dominance to climbing-galaxias dominance around 1980–83, the 1988–91 surveys finding Pedder at only 5 of 117 sites, the 68 fish captured from the two Bonnet Bay creeks in 43 trips, and the last wild capture in 1996 followed by further unsuccessful surveys. Once you have that, Codex can calibrate the simulator by pattern-matching rather than by pretending the data are richer than they are; simulation-based Bayesian calibration, such as approximate Bayesian computation, would fit that philosophy quite well.  ([Tasmanian DNR&E][1])

On parameters, I would borrow Pedder baseline demography from the translocated populations and captive-breeding/life-history work, trout demography from Tasmanian fishery material, and climbing-galaxias demography from Tasmanian and perhaps New Zealand studies. ALA may be useful at the margins for comparative occurrence patterns, but I suspect the real gold will be in Inland Fisheries archives, stocking records, museum specimens, and any old catch-per-unit-effort or stomach-content data. One nice feature of the system is that the translocated Pedder populations at Lake Oberon and Strathgordon at least give you some handle on intrinsic Pedder dynamics away from the original invasion setting, even if they are not a perfect proxy. ([Tasmanian DNR&E][1])

The outputs I’d aim for are not a single blame percentage, but relative support for four competing models: trout-only, climbing-only, both, and both-plus-habitat-change; extinction-risk surfaces showing where Pedder persistence becomes implausible across a grid of trout and climbing pressures; and counterfactual runs for management questions such as no trout stocking, slower climbing colonisation, trout suppression only, climbing suppression only, and both suppressed. I’d also insist on a proper global sensitivity analysis, because in a data-poor system robustness matters as much as fit. My guess is that absolute historical densities will be too uncertain for a defensible “fish per hectare” threshold, so I’d promise thresholds in standardised pressure units or CPUE-equivalent units unless the archival data turn out to be unexpectedly strong. If the combined model is the only one that reproduces both the early decline and the later replacement pattern, that is already a good result, and it would mesh with the long-standing view that Lake Pedder itself is not a sensible reintroduction target while trout and climbing galaxias remain entrenched there.  ([DCCEEW][2])

The Clarence Lagoon idea strikes me as the cleaner management-optimisation problem. There the control lever is explicit—brook trout stocking density—and the biological question is coexistence, not retrospective extirpation. Official recovery material says Clarence Lagoon has been stocked with brook trout since 1963, that Clarence galaxias has remained relatively abundant there, and that brook trout were deliberately maintained as a comparatively benign alternative to brown trout. That makes it ideal for a forward model that maximises angler benefit subject to a hard galaxias persistence constraint. I’d see that as either a pilot for the Pedder code base or a neat companion paper, but Pedder is the stronger headline story. ([Tasmanian DNR&E][3])

If it were me, I’d probably do the engine in Python because a custom simulator, calibration wrapper, scenario sweeps, and testable modules suit that workflow well; R would still be perfectly good if you want a stats-first workflow and more familiar ecological analysis conventions. Either way, I’d keep the repo very simple: one cleaned evidence table, one parameter-prior table, one scenario file, one simulator, one calibration script, one sensitivity-analysis script, and one reporting notebook. In one sentence, the project I’d pitch is: can a mechanistic stochastic model, calibrated to sparse historical evidence, distinguish whether brown trout, climbing galaxias, or their combination best explains the extirpation of Pedder galaxias from Lake Pedder? That feels both feasible and genuinely interesting.

## New framing to test (March 2026; from Chris Burridge)

A further hypothesis worth testing is whether Pedder galaxias was driven extinct by a transient "perfect storm" created by inundation, terrestrial trophic subsidy, and the trout-stocking pulse, rather than by a permanent impossibility of coexistence with trout and climbing galaxias. In this framing, post-flood productivity plus the 1972 stocking may have produced unusually large, highly piscivorous brown trout that overshot any long-run coexistence envelope with *G. pedderensis*. If so, the historical extinction event may say more about an exceptional early-impoundment regime than about the present-day lake under current conditions.

That opens a useful extra counterfactual: not just "what caused the original extirpation?" but also "are the causal conditions that produced extirpation still present?" In model terms, this suggests separating transient drivers from persistent ones. The transient drivers would include the short-lived flooding subsidy, an initial trout overshoot, and perhaps a temporary size-structure effect that elevated piscivory. The persistent drivers would include continued trout presence, climbing galaxias presence, altered littoral habitat, and refuge-stream dynamics.

This framing is attractive because it is more novel than the default reintroduction narrative. Most failed reintroductions occur because the original threatening process was not removed. Here, the more interesting question may be whether Lake Pedder experienced a one-off extinction trap that no longer exists in the same form. The model could therefore compare at least three management interpretations: historical extirpation was inevitable under any trout-plus-brev scenario; historical extirpation required the special post-flood and post-stocking overshoot conditions; or present-day coexistence is still implausible because persistent threat levels remain above a viability threshold.

Two other additions are worth capturing now. First, *Galaxias parvus* was also present in the system and coexisted with *pedderensis*, so it should at least appear in the conceptual background even if it ends up being a minor player in the model. Second, Charlotte's 2025 *Galaxias johnstoni* paper and Kaleigh's *pedderensis* genetic study sound highly valuable for the Introduction because they provide both trout-versus-galaxiid context and a reconstructed grey-literature history of the Pedder system. They may also sharpen the contrast between fragmentation caused before trout arrival and fragmentation driven by trout invasion.

The new narrative draft also usefully sharpens some wording for the paper itself. "Trophic upsurge" is probably the right ecological term for the early post-inundation productivity pulse, and the note that Pedder abundance appears to have peaked around 1977 may give us a more explicit target pattern than the looser "early boom" wording. Likewise, the 1996 "one lonely individual" formulation is rhetorically strong but also scientifically helpful because it reminds us that the final natural-range record can be treated as a point observation near functional extirpation rather than just another generic survey year.

There is also a potentially important empirical side-question around identity and conservation target. If climbing galaxias occasionally invades refuge systems and if there is any risk of hybridisation or mito-nuclear discordance, then the project may eventually need a short genetics appendix or at least a framing paragraph on taxonomic integrity. The current notes mention mitochondrial mixing, allozymes suggesting separation, and the possibility of checking this with DArT or RAD-style data. That is probably not a phase-1 modelling task, but it is worth parking as a live issue because it affects how confidently we interpret both historical material and current refuge populations.

## Literature-ingestion update (April 2026)

- The newly ingested literature strengthens the `trophic upsurge` framing and makes `1977` the best current candidate year for the post-inundation Pedder peak.
- That `1977` target is now supported well enough to use in calibration, but the direct Pedder-only fish time series is still one step removed. Chilcott and the 2025 genetics paper both cite it clearly, while Lake 2001 directly documents the accompanying littoral-fauna peak.
- The transient `perfect storm` framing has become stronger, not weaker: early enrichment plus trout overshoot plus later climbing-galaxias dominance is now the most interesting causal sequence to test.
- `Galaxias parvus` should stay in the conceptual community background because the primary literature suggests it persisted at much higher levels than `pedderensis` after inundation.
- Genetics and introgression now deserve a defined sidecar slot. The new Pedder genetics paper shows evidence of `brevipinnis` introgression into `pedderensis`, but the best-supported timing appears to pre-date 1972, so this should not be forced into phase-1 demographic collapse modeling as a primary mechanism.
- The better paper framing is now a two-part question:
- which mechanism set best explains the historical collapse
- whether the conditions that caused collapse are still present now, or whether extinction reflected a transient overshoot regime that may no longer exist
