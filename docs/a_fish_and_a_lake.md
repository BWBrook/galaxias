# A story about a fish and a lake

Just a little way off in the distance is the Lake Pedder basin. For those who are unaware, this is now Tasmania’s second-largest lake, but it was once very different. It was originally a natural glacial lake 300m above sea level with dark waters ringed by a distinctive, pink quartzite beach (see figure below). However, in 1972 Tasmania’s Hydro Electric Commission dammed the Serpentine and Huon rivers to improve electricity supply, flooding the existing lake, devastating the surrounding ecosystem, and creating the much larger body of water that exists today. The decision to flood Lake Pedder caused a fierce, years-long environmental battle that would have both national and international consequences. The area around Lake Pedder had been a National Park since 1955, but its status was abruptly revoked with then-Tasmanian Premier Eric Reece saying the park "wasn’t of substantial significance in the scheme of things”. I’ll let you decide for yourself.

The quartzite beach (up to 800 m from scrub to water’s edge) was lost, and the surrounding marshy habitat was drowned. But for the star of our show, the Pedder galaxias (*Galaxias pedderensis*), things were also changing.

## The Pedder galaxias

The Pedder galaxias is a small (<130 mm) freshwater fish that was first described in 1968 and found only in Lake Pedder. Before the construction of the new lake, this species preferred Lake Pedder itself, which was quite shallow and surrounded by white sandy beaches and small rooted aquatic vegetation. However, adults were also found in swamps and low velocity streams with abundant instream organic debris and overhanging terrestrial vegetation. It fed on small invertebrates, particularly aquatic insect larvae and crustaceans. It was considered abundant. However, with the flooding came dramatic ecosystem change.

## Boom times?

Following the artificial raising of this lake, connectivity with the Huon catchment was established (see figure below). This enabled brown trout (*Salmo trutta*) and climbing galaxias (*Galaxias brevipinnis*) to invade Lake Pedder for the first time. Furthermore, just to make sure, 350,000 hatchery-raised brown trout juveniles were also stocked in 1972. But despite this ‘invasion’, the Pedder galaxias thrived! This is a common observation associated with the raising of lake levels, as the inundated terrestrial habitat provides a massive increase in nutrients and food for fish, otherwise known as ‘trophic upsurge’. Pedder galaxias abundance peaked in 1977, 5 years after damming. However, 10 years later, Pedder galaxias were all but gone. What happened?

## What went wrong?

Brown trout were documented to consume *G. pedderensis* during the 1970’s and 1980’s, possibly explaining the ‘trophy’ sized trout fished during this period. In the meantime, the Pedder galaxias was declining. In fact, one of the first groups to voice concern was the trout fishermen! During this period, a famous mainland Australian limnologist, Sam Lake (yes, that was his name), was documenting the changes to Pedder’s fauna, and raised the plight of the Pedder galaxias. By the late 1980’s the trout were returning to ‘normal’ size, and authorities started searching for Pedder galaxias, and what did they find? Well, plenty of galaxiids, but the ‘wrong’ kind: *Galaxias brevipinnis*!

At the same time as trout were chomping down on Pedder galaxias, the climbing galaxias was also increasing markedly in abundance. This species can co-exist (albeit at reduced abundance) with brown trout. Their ‘climbing’ ability enables them to exploit streams inaccessible to brown trout, and hence persist. The climbing galaxias also has a history of displacing other native galaxiid fishes, so it formed a two-pronged attack on Pedder galaxias with trout. The low-flow adapted Pedder galaxias had nowhere to hide; the flooding of the low relief lake margins had also dramatically decreased the abundance of small gentle streams in which they might find some relief. Instead, they were challenged to live alongside climbing galaxiids on an unequal footing.

## Management on the run

The abundance of Pedder galaxias declined so rapidly and dramatically there was little opportunity to even gather basic information about its biology. As might be imagined in such dire circumstances, what few remaining individuals that could be sourced from the wild were used in attempts at captive breeding. These had limited success. Then, as a last-ditch effort, a translocation of 42 individuals was made to nearby Lake Oberon in 1991/2. This much smaller lake was deemed beyond the reach of trout and climbing galaxias, perched in the Western Arthur Range at 800m. However, breeding was not confirmed there until several years later. Surveys continued in Lake Pedder until 2002, but the last time Pedder galaxias was observed was 1996—one lonely individual. Pedder galaxias became officially listed as ‘Extinct in the wild’, owing to the lack of definitive proof for its existence in its native habitat (Lake Pedder), although the IUCN considers it ‘Endangered’. Could they still be out there in the lake? People are still looking for Thylacines out here!

Putting all your eggs in one basket is not ideal, so with the Lake Oberon population doing well by the early 2000’s, this population was used to establish another, located much closer to our travels. While by no means a closely guarded secret, but still widely unknown, the drinking water supply dam for Strathgordon now harbours what is the second remaining population of Pedder galaxias. Hopefully the residents don’t get too thirsty! This population is monitored annually, and apart from the occasional incursion by climbing galaxias, it seems to be doing well. However, some people question the philosophy of maintaining a species outside its native range. Furthermore, our friend the climbing galaxias has a habit of hybridising with other galaxiids, so can we be sure what we are conserving? Likewise, how many Lake Oberon endemics may have been forced to extinction in the process? Were other species lost from Lake Pedder? Most certainly yes. How many? Who knows? But somewhat surprisingly another galaxiid did survive, the swamp galaxias (*Galaxias parvus*), so let’s end on that positive note.
