## Pedder chronology and life-history extraction

### Pre-flood baseline

- Frankenberg (1968) confirms that `G. pedderensis` was described from 1965-1967 collections in and near original Lake Pedder, especially near the Lake Maria inflow, and that it was abundant near the lake edge and in the inflowing stream.
- Frankenberg also places the species only in Lake Pedder and its immediate surroundings at the time of description, which is a strong pre-flood range anchor.
- The same paper confirms that `G. parvus` co-occurred with `G. pedderensis`, supporting Chris Burridge's suggestion that `parvus` belongs in the community background even if it is not a phase-1 modeled state variable.

### Flooding and early boom

- The inundation sequence is now clearer: the impoundment was created through 1972-1974 and joined formerly separate fish assemblages.
- Lake (2001) is the cleanest mechanistic source for the post-flood productivity pulse. It shows a large littoral-fauna boom from 1975 to 1977, with mean total animals per sampled site peaking in 1977 and then declining strongly by 1989.
- Chilcott et al. (2013) explicitly connect that early-system boom to both endemic galaxiids, stating that sweep-net surveys showed Pedder and swamp galaxias increasing after inundation and peaking in 1977.
- Horner et al. (2025) repeats the 1977 peak signal and ties it to the broader post-flood rise in food availability.

### Decline sequence

- The chronology remains strongly consistent across the best sources:
- `1975-1980`: Pedder galaxias present in large numbers in the new impoundment and some tributaries.
- `1977`: strongest current candidate year for peak post-flood abundance.
- `By 1978`: secondary sources report the decline was already noticeable.
- `Early 1980s`: Pedder galaxias became exceptionally rare.
- `By about 1983`: climbing galaxias had become the dominant galaxiid in the altered system.
- `1988-1989`: targeted electrofishing confirmed absence from the impoundment and persistence only in four small inflowing streams.
- `1990-1991`: 117 sites surveyed, fewer than 80 fish found, 68 from the two Bonnet Bay streams.
- `1992-1996`: only 10 individuals collected during further extensive surveys.
- `6 March 1996`: exact last natural-range record in the official recovery-plan wording.
- `1998-2002`: annual surveys continued with no further detections.

### Life-history extraction

- Hamr (1995) gives the cleanest short summary of the remaining refuge habitat: lower meandering stream sections, up to about 2 km upstream, with deep pools, slow flow, sandy substrate, abundant cover, and dense overhanging vegetation.
- Hamr (1995) also consolidates the main life-history parameters already suggested by official summaries: freshwater-only life cycle, spring spawning, relatively low fecundity, and large eggs.
- The existing simulator plan remains well matched to these traits: annual or seasonal stage structure, strong sensitivity to spawning/recruitment disruption, and a lake plus refuge-stream compartment structure.

## Threat-mechanism extraction

### Trophic upsurge

- The literature bundle strongly supports standardizing on `trophic upsurge` for the early post-inundation boom.
- Lake (2001) gives the direct ecological basis: drowned vegetation, peat, detritus, filamentous algae, invertebrate increase, and the coincident period of very large trout.
- This is the best present support for a transient state variable that decays after inundation rather than a permanent habitat improvement.

### Trout

- The one-off `350,000` brown trout fry release in 1972 is now supported by Chilcott et al. (2013), Horner et al. (2025), and earlier official material.
- Chilcott et al. (2013) is clearer than our earlier web-only pass that the trout population increase was probably not dependent on stocking alone, because the new impoundment also created abundant spawning opportunity.
- The early large-trout phase is now better grounded as part of the causal story, not just a colorful anecdote. Lake (2001), Chilcott et al. (2013), Horner et al. (2025), and De Vos (2019) all line up on a short-lived high-productivity period that favored trophy trout.
- The strongest current inference is still that trout likely initiated the sharp decline through predation, with possible competition layered on top.

### Climbing galaxias

- The newer literature keeps reinforcing the same sequence already visible in Sanger (2001): `G. brevipinnis` became abundant later than the initial Pedder decline, but likely accelerated collapse and prevented recovery.
- Horner et al. (2025) sharpens the mechanistic possibilities: higher fecundity, strong climbing ability, access to habitats less available to trout, and likely combined competition and predation risk.
- This supports keeping `brevipinnis` as an explicit modeled pressure and not collapsing the problem into trout-only versus habitat-only.

### Habitat-only explanation

- The bundle does not rescue a habitat-only explanation as the main story.
- Habitat loss clearly mattered because the original slow, lowland, meandering littoral-stream mosaic was destroyed.
- But the stronger reading after this review is still `habitat change plus biotic invasion`, not habitat change in isolation.

## Recovery/translocation chronology

### Captive breeding

- Hamr (1992) and Chilcott et al. (2013) align closely on the basic outcome: captive breeding and artificial fertilisation were attempted urgently but produced only `11` juveniles, which was nowhere near enough to serve as the primary recovery pathway.
- The practical implication for the project is that captive breeding is part of the historical contingency story, not a realistic demographic source term for the native system.

### Lake Oberon

- Hamr (1992) remains central for site-selection logic. Lake Oberon was chosen because it combined fish-free status, an inflowing stream thought suitable for spawning, suitable water chemistry and temperature, abundant food, and relative isolation from invasion.
- Chilcott et al. (2013) provides the cleanest establishment chronology after release:
- `31` fish released in 1991-1992
- `3` more in 1997
- adult detected in 1996 but establishment still uncertain
- adults, juveniles, and a postlarval fish documented in 1997
- strong increase thereafter
- By 2012, Chilcott et al. (2013) reports `158` fish in fyke nets plus `27` in the inflow stream during a limited survey area, implying a total population in the low thousands.

### Strathgordon

- The early Strathgordon phase remains best treated as two linked stages:
- `2001-2002`: initial transfer of `74` adults
- `2001-2007`: total of `353` fish transferred in five events
- Chilcott et al. (2013) is the cleanest source for the full `353` count and year-by-year transfer breakdown.
- The important mechanistic point is the recruitment lag. No recruitment was detected until `2008`, after shoreline rock placement in `2007` improved spawning habitat.
- This is a strong empirical reminder that habitat suitability for spawning can dominate short-run outcomes even in predator-free systems.

### Genetics and insurance-population interpretation

- Horner et al. (2025) materially upgrades the insurance-population story.
- Lake Oberon and Strathgordon retain similar heterozygosity and show low genetic differentiation.
- This means the serial translocation did not obviously strip away the remaining standing variation.
- The paper also reinforces that Strathgordon is more exposed to future risk than Oberon because of proximity to threat species, even though it is currently isolated by barriers.

## Conflicting claims and unresolved discrepancies

### Founding numbers for Lake Oberon

- Sanger (2001): `42` fish released between November 1991 and November 1992.
- Recovery Plan 2006 and Chilcott et al. (2013): `34` fish transferred between 1991 and 1997, specifically `31` in 1991-1992 and `3` in 1997.
- Horner et al. (2025): about `45` individuals between 1991 and 1997, including `7` captive-bred descendants.
- Working interpretation: these are probably different counting conventions rather than simple error. Some sources appear to count only wild-caught fish successfully transferred, some count the 1997 top-up separately, and some include captive-bred descendants. We should preserve all three claims in the ledger until original translocation logs or Gaffney et al. (1992) are obtained.

### Serial translocation counts into Strathgordon

- EPBC and Recovery Plan wording: `74` adults moved in `2001-2002`.
- Chilcott et al. (2013) and Horner et al. (2025): `353` fish in five transfers between `2001` and `2007`.
- Working interpretation: this is not a contradiction so much as an early-phase subset versus the full multi-year program. For modeling, `74` is the early establishment input and `353` is the total serial founding count.

### Wording around the last 1996 natural-range record

- Recovery Plan 2006 gives the strongest wording: no Pedder galaxias sighted or collected since `6 March 1996`, despite annual surveys until `2002`.
- Chilcott et al. (2013) also ties the last record to `6 March 1996`.
- Horner et al. (2025) says the last `G. pedderensis` was observed in the lake during `1996`.
- Working wording for the project should be:
- `The last record from the natural Lake Pedder system was on 6 March 1996; annual surveys continued to 2002 with no further detections.`
- This avoids overclaiming `lake proper` versus `tributary refuge` when sources use slightly different shorthand.

### Whether the 1977 peak is directly supported or still indirect

- Support is now strong enough to use `1977` as a calibration target.
- Chilcott et al. (2013) explicitly states that sweep-net surveys showed both endemic Galaxias species peaking in `1977`.
- Horner et al. (2025) repeats the `1977` peak claim.
- Lake (2001) directly documents the invertebrate and littoral-fauna peak in `1977`, and links this interval to the trophy-trout phase.
- Remaining caution: we still do not have the original fish-specific abundance series or a clean Pedder-only numeric time series from Lake's primary data products. So the `1977 peak` is well supported qualitatively and semi-directly, but not yet pinned to a dedicated Pedder abundance table.

## Comparative trout-galaxiid mechanism takeaways

- Ault and White (1994) is a very valuable Tasmania-based proxy. After quantitatively accounting for habitat structure, brown trout still explained strong reductions in `G. truttaceus` density. This supports treating trout effects as more than a habitat confound.
- Townsend and Crowl (1991) remains one of the strongest exclusion-pattern papers for our purposes: `69` trout-only sites, `63` galaxiid-only sites, and only `9` co-occurrence sites, with galaxiids mostly above trout-blocking waterfalls. It is especially useful for supporting a trout-driven fragmentation mechanism.
- Glova and Sagar (1991) is useful mainly as a `limited coexistence under juvenile trout` paper rather than as evidence of harmless overlap. `G. brevipinnis` fed mostly at night and in slightly faster water than juvenile trout, which reduced but did not eliminate overlap.
- Glova and Sagar (1993) sharpens the size-structure point. Coexistence with juvenile salmonids in coarse-substrate streams can occur, but the authors argue predation becomes more important where larger trout are present and bottom cover is poorer.
- Hardie et al. (2006) is the cleanest Tasmanian statewide synthesis for our Introduction. It treats exotic fish, especially salmonids, as the dominant current threat class for many galaxiids and explicitly says that coexistence with salmonids needs more careful examination rather than simple assumption.
- McDowall (2006) is the broad comparative review that makes the strongest general case that southern cool-temperate galaxiids have repeatedly suffered major declines after salmonid introductions, often leaving communities where displacement is no longer directly visible because co-occurrence has already been broken down.

## Genetics and introgression implications

- Horner et al. (2025) is enough to move genetics from a vague side-question to a real sidecar work package.
- The paper supports real introgression between `G. pedderensis` and `G. brevipinnis`, but not necessarily recent introgression caused by the 1972 inundation.
- The best-supported model places introgression well before anthropogenic contact, although a recent-contact-only model is not strongly rejected.
- This means introgression should not be built into phase-1 demographic collapse modeling as a required extinction driver.
- It should instead sit in the project as a conservation-integrity and future-management issue:
- what exactly counts as the target taxon
- whether the current insurance populations preserve that target adequately
- whether additional translocations should consider genetic mixing, rescue, or screening

- Jense et al. (2025) is extremely useful framing support because it shows that present-day allopatry with trout does not automatically imply trout caused fragmentation.
- Waters et al. (2023) pushes the same caution further: contemporary distributions can mislead about past contact and introgression history, especially where later trout-driven fragmentation has obscured older contacts.
- Together, these papers argue for a careful split in our own framing:
- demographic collapse can still be due to trout and `brevipinnis`
- but genetic structure or introgression patterns cannot be read naively off present distributions

## Paper-writing takeaways for Introduction framing

- The best framing remains a retrospective causal-attribution problem, not a courtroom-style single-culprit paper.
- The most novel angle is now clearer: was Lake Pedder a transient `perfect storm` driven by trophic upsurge, trout overshoot, and later climbing-galaxias dominance, rather than evidence of a timeless impossibility of coexistence?
- `Trophic upsurge` should now be the default ecological term for the early post-inundation boom.
- The `1977 peak` is now strong enough to treat as a named target pattern in model calibration, while still flagging that the direct Pedder-only abundance series is not yet in hand.
- `Galaxias parvus` should stay in the paper as community background and as a useful reminder that not all endemic galaxiids in the system responded identically.
- Genetics and introgression should be handled as a sidecar question or appendix-level issue unless later evidence shows direct demographic consequences.
- The comparative literature gives us a nice contrast class:
- some galaxiids clearly collapse or fragment in the presence of trout
- some can coexist under specific habitat and size-structure conditions
- present-day allopatry does not by itself prove trout caused the historical split

- That combination makes the Pedder project stronger, not weaker. It lets us ask both:
- which mechanism set best explains the historical collapse
- whether the conditions that caused the collapse are still present now
